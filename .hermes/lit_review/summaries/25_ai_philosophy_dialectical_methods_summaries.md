# Subtopic 25 — AI Philosophy, Dialectical Methods, and Scholastic-Style Reasoning

1. **Murray Shanahan, “Talking About Large Language Models” (2022/2023).** Contribution: this conceptual paper argues that LLMs should be understood as simulators of language-conditioned behavior rather than casually anthropomorphized as agents with stable beliefs, knowledge, or intentions; its central evidence is a technical-philosophical account of how fluent language invites misleading human interpretations. Limitation: it offers no generation protocol, philosophical benchmark, or empirical test of scholastic reasoning. Direct novelty threat: it weakens any claim that a system has newly created an “AI philosopher” merely because it produces convincing philosophical discourse, but it does not pre-empt a typed disputation engine. Evaluation relevance: separate role or voice consistency from argument validity and test prompt paraphrase, persona reversal, and adversarial framing controls. Source: [arXiv:2212.03551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03551).

2. **Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda, and Sanmi Koyejo, “Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?” (2023).** Contribution: the paper shows mathematically and empirically that apparently discontinuous scaling abilities can be produced by nonlinear metrics, thresholding, and reporting choices rather than by a genuine capability transition; it supports this with analyses of InstructGPT/GPT-3 tasks, BIG-Bench, and vision models. Limitation: it studies general scaling claims, not philosophical reasoning or structured dialogue, and does not prescribe a scholastic evaluation rubric. Direct novelty threat: it directly challenges broad claims that a prompt protocol “creates reasoning” based only on a jump in final-answer accuracy or debate win rate. Evaluation relevance: use continuous obligation-level scores, confidence intervals, calibration, metric-sensitivity analyses, and budget-matched comparisons rather than relying on coarse aggregate success. Source: [arXiv:2304.15004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004).

3. **Eric Schwitzgebel, David Schwitzgebel, and Anna Strasser, “Creating a Large Language Model of a Philosopher” (2023).** Contribution: the authors fine-tune GPT-3 on Daniel Dennett’s writings, ask Dennett and the model the same ten questions, and collect 425 judgments about which answers were human-authored; experts identified Dennett’s responses at 51%, above the 20% chance level but below the preregistered 80% expectation, while ordinary participants were near chance and some model answers were preferred. Limitation: the experiment measures stylistic or positional indistinguishability in a small question set, not validity, provenance, objection coverage, or dialectical structure. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts broad claims that generating recognizable philosophical positions or passing philosopher-authorship discrimination is itself novel. Evaluation relevance: add blinded expert discrimination, position fidelity, question-level variance, historical attribution checks, and separate style imitation from argument quality. Source: [arXiv:2302.01339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01339).

4. **Hangfeng He, Hongming Zhang, and Dan Roth, “SocREval: Large Language Models with the Socratic Method for Reference-free Reasoning Evaluation” (Findings of NAACL 2024).** Contribution: SocREval uses a Socratic-method-inspired prompt to have GPT-4 evaluate reasoning chains without requiring a gold reasoning chain, and reports improved performance over reference-free and reference-based metrics on four human-annotated datasets, with analyses of cost, prompt robustness, and example selection. Limitation: the method evaluates reasoning-chain quality rather than generating a philosophical disputation, and its questions do not encode *objection*, *sed contra*, determination, distinction, or reply roles. Direct novelty threat: it is a direct precedent for sequential interrogation of intermediate reasoning, so novelty cannot rest on Socratic questioning alone. Evaluation relevance: adapt its staged questioning idea to audit each scholastic obligation, but validate the evaluator against expert annotations and test judge bias and self-preference. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-naacl.175/).

5. **Shunyu Yao et al., “Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models” (2023).** Contribution: Tree of Thoughts generalizes chain-of-thought into branching units of thought with self-evaluation, lookahead, pruning, and backtracking; on Game of 24, creative writing, and mini-crosswords, it demonstrates that explicit search over intermediate states can substantially improve task performance, including a reported GPT-4 increase from 4% to 74% on Game of 24. Limitation: the tasks are puzzles and planning problems, and the framework has no philosophical ontology, authority handling, or expert evaluation of distinctions and replies. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts claims that branching intermediate reasoning or search is novel. Evaluation relevance: compare semantic scholastic branches against generic ToT under equal model-call, token, latency, and retrieval budgets, and measure whether objections are distinct, relevant, and resolved rather than merely alternative thoughts. Source: [arXiv:2305.10601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601).

6. **Yilun Du et al., “Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate” (2023).** Contribution: the paper has multiple instances independently propose answers, debate their reasoning over several rounds, and converge on a final answer; it reports gains in mathematical and strategic reasoning and reduced factual errors and hallucinations across evaluated tasks. Limitation: agents are not assigned scholastic semantic types, and the method does not require a fixed question, explicit contrary authority, determination, or one reply mapped to every objection. Direct novelty threat: it is the closest established threat to claims that iterative objection/rebuttal exchange itself improves reasoning. Evaluation relevance: include it as a primary baseline and measure objection coverage, correction rate, reply alignment, final accuracy, and marginal quality per model call. Source: [arXiv:2305.14325](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325).

7. **Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, and Dario Amodei, “AI Safety via Debate” (2018).** Contribution: this foundational proposal formulates two-agent, turn-taking debate with a human or overseer judging which side provides more truthful and useful information, gives a complexity-theoretic motivation, and reports an initial MNIST experiment in which debate improved classifier accuracy. Limitation: its objective is scalable oversight and winner selection, not clarification through a fixed philosophical question structure, and its initial empirical setting is narrow. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts generic claims about adversarial two-sided debate, human judging, or exposing flaws through contestation. Evaluation relevance: borrow judge-reliability, hidden-information, and adversarial-robustness tests while explicitly checking that debate victory is not being conflated with truth, validity, or scholastic compliance. Source: [arXiv:1805.00899](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00899).

8. **Chi-Min Chan et al., “ChatEval: Towards Better LLM-based Evaluators through Multi-Agent Debate” (ICLR 2024).** Contribution: ChatEval builds a multi-agent referee team with role-diverse prompts that debates candidate texts and aggregates the judgments; experiments on two benchmarks report improved accuracy and correlation with human assessment, and show that diverse roles matter more than repeating one role. Limitation: the target is preference or quality agreement, so the system may reward fluency, persuasion, or style without verifying individual premises and inferences. Direct novelty threat: it directly challenges novelty claims about role-specialized evaluator debate or multi-agent LLM judging. Evaluation relevance: compare against role-diverse judging, report criterion-level human agreement, and add adversarial cases such as fluent invalid arguments, verbosity bias, duplicated objections, and fabricated authorities. Source: [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=FQepisCUWu).

9. **Tian Liang et al., “Encouraging Divergent Thinking in Large Language Models through Multi-Agent Debate” (2023; ICLR-era work).** Contribution: this paper identifies a degeneration-of-thought problem in iterative self-reflection and proposes multi-agent debate in which agents exchange opposing arguments under a judge, reporting improvements on commonsense machine translation and counter-intuitive arithmetic; it also finds that adaptive stopping and moderate debate depth matter and that mixed-model judging can be unfair. Limitation: the method seeks diversity and better answers but does not define scholastic roles or require authority, distinctions, determination, or objection-specific replies. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts claims that a system’s novelty lies simply in generating divergent objections through multiple agents. Evaluation relevance: score objection diversity, non-redundancy, relevance, and adversarial strength separately, and test judge fairness when agents or judges differ by model family. Source: [arXiv:2305.19118](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118).

10. **Yunxuan Li et al., “Improving Multi-Agent Debate with Sparse Communication Topology” (Findings of EMNLP 2024).** Contribution: the authors study how communication connectivity affects multi-agent debate and report that sparse topologies can match or outperform fully connected debate while reducing computational cost on GPT and Mistral models; they also extend the setup to multimodal reasoning and alignment labeling. Limitation: topology is treated as a systems variable, not as a semantic representation of objection, authority, distinction, or reply, and the benchmarks are not philosophical. Direct novelty threat: it weakens claims that specialized communication channels or agent organization are novel without a semantic protocol. Evaluation relevance: use sparse-topology debate as an efficiency baseline and compare channels by proof-relevant role, coverage, latency, token cost, and quality per call. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.427/).

11. **Reza Sanayei, Srdjan Vesic, Eduardo Blanco, and Mihai Surdeanu, “Can LLMs Judge Debates? Evaluating Non-Linear Reasoning via Argumentation Theory Semantics” (Findings of EMNLP 2025).** Contribution: the paper converts natural-language debates into argument-graph problems and tests whether LLMs can approximate Quantitative Argumentation Debate semantics, which assigns acceptability from attack and support relations; models show moderate alignment but degrade with longer inputs or disrupted discourse flow, while advanced prompting reduces length and position biases. Limitation: the graph lacks scholastic distinctions among authority, determination, and replies, and the models receive dialogue rather than the underlying graph. Direct novelty threat: it is a direct precedent for graph-level, non-linear debate evaluation and therefore limits claims that argument-relation auditing is unprecedented. Evaluation relevance: add attack/support consistency, graph-level acceptability, length and order perturbations, and graph-aware versus dialogue-only ablations to objection–reply evaluation. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.1159/).

12. **Weiqi Wang and Yangqiu Song, “MARS: Benchmarking the Metaphysical Reasoning Abilities of Language Models with a Multi-task Evaluation Dataset” (2024).** Contribution: MARS formulates metaphysical reasoning as a three-step discriminative process concerning action changes, resulting state changes, and situational transitions, then evaluates 20 language models and finds all three tasks difficult even for strong systems; conceptualization-taxonomy pretraining is reported as a possible aid. Limitation: MARS is a domain benchmark rather than a dialectical generator, and its tasks do not evaluate objections, authorities, distinctions, determinations, or replies. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts claims that philosophy-specific reasoning evaluation is novel merely because generic benchmarks are replaced with philosophical content. Evaluation relevance: use its task-family design as a model for philosophy-specific datasets, then add expert-labeled disputation stages, counterfactuals, and proof obligations. Source: [arXiv:2406.02106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02106).

13. **Ramon Ruiz-Dolz, Zlata Kikteva, and John Lawrence, “Mining Complex Patterns of Argumentative Reasoning in Natural Language Dialogue” (ACL 2025).** Contribution: the paper introduces QT-Schemes, a corpus of 441 dialogue arguments annotated with 24 argumentation schemes, and evaluates Transformer and LLM approaches for mining scheme structure from textbook-like and real-world argumentation. Limitation: scheme mining identifies stereotyped inference patterns but does not establish proof validity, implement a disputation workflow, or guarantee that all argumentative units receive a scheme label. Direct novelty threat: it is the closest representation precedent for a typed argumentative ontology and directly threatens claims that structured argument-form extraction is new. Evaluation relevance: adapt its annotation strategy for scholastic roles and report macro-F1, premise/conclusion relations, provenance, and transfer from natural dialogue to expert-authored disputations. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.368/).

14. **Elfia Bezou-Vrakatseli, Oana Cocarascu, and Sanjay Modgil, “Can Large Language Models Understand Argument Schemes?” (Findings of ACL 2025).** Contribution: this systematic study evaluates seven LLMs on Walton-style argument-scheme classification under zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting, including manually annotated and generated enthymemes, and compares formal definitions with model-generated descriptions; larger models perform satisfactorily in some settings while smaller models remain challenged. Limitation: classification of argument form is not end-to-end reasoning, proof validity, or successful reply generation, and performance varies by scheme and prompting regime. Direct novelty threat: it directly pre-empts claims that LLM recognition of argumentative form is itself novel. Evaluation relevance: include scheme/form classification as an upstream metric, test macro-F1 and cross-domain robustness, and measure whether correct scheme recognition predicts objection quality or downstream answer success. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.702/).

15. **Yisong Miao et al., “Discursive Socratic Questioning: Evaluating the Faithfulness of Language Models’ Understanding of Discourse Relations” (ACL 2024).** Contribution: DiSQ evaluates discourse understanding through sequences of targeted, counterfactual, and consistency questions rather than a single classification score; on PDTB, the best GPT model reaches only 41% of ideal performance, while the method remains robust to domain shifts and paraphrase variations. Limitation: it tests discourse-relation faithfulness, not philosophical validity, authority fidelity, or compliance with scholastic roles. Direct novelty threat: it is a direct precedent for multi-question faithfulness auditing and weakens claims that sequential cross-question consistency tests are new. Evaluation relevance: apply targeted, counterfactual, and consistency probes to ensure a determination remains stable across replies, detect equivocation, and distinguish superficial agreement from preserved reasoning relations. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.341/).

16. **Chuyi Kong et al., “PlatoLM: Teaching LLMs in Multi-Round Dialogue via a User Simulator” (ACL 2024).** Contribution: PlatoLM introduces a Socratic user simulator trained from questions extracted from genuine human–machine conversations and reports state-of-the-art MT-Bench performance among LLaMA-based 7B models, with more human-like questioning patterns and richer topic structures than prior simulated-dialogue approaches. Limitation: it targets realistic multi-turn dialogue training and response quality, not philosophical argument validity, typed objections, authorities, distinctions, or objection coverage. Direct novelty threat: it pre-empts broad claims about using a Socratic simulator or multi-round questioning as such, but not claims about executable scholastic disputation semantics. Evaluation relevance: treat dialogue quality and questioning realism as separate baselines, then evaluate whether a scholastic protocol adds auditable intermediate obligations beyond conversational naturalness. Source: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.424/).

## Cross-paper conclusion

Across the verified corpus, the strongest threats cover generic debate, multi-agent judging, branching search, Socratic interrogation, argument-scheme recognition, graph-based debate evaluation, and philosophy-specific benchmarking. None of these inspected papers combines a typed and auditable sequence of question, objections, *sed contra* authority, determination, distinctions, and one-to-one replies with expert-annotated evaluation of those intermediate obligations. The defensible novelty boundary is therefore the integrated scholastic protocol and its proof-relevant evaluation—not LLM debate, philosophical text generation, Socratic questioning, structured reasoning, or argument mining in isolation. This remains a plausible but unproven claim until budget-matched ablations, expert annotations, provenance checks, and judge-bias controls are run.
