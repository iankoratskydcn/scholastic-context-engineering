# Subtopic 23 — Disputatio, Dialectical Dialogue, and Objection/Reply AI

## 1. Uckelman (2013), “Medieval Disputationes de obligationibus as Formal Dialogue Systems”
Structure: The paper treats a medieval *obligatio* as an alternating dialogue between an Opponent and a Respondent, beginning from a positum and governed by rules for admitting, denying, or responding to propositions. Method: Uckelman reconstructs one medieval obligation theory in Prakken’s formal dialogue-system framework and gives it a Dynamic Epistemic Logic semantics, including dialogue states and conditions for consistency or successful response. Finding: The analysis shows that medieval obligational disputation can be represented as an executable formal dialogue system rather than merely described historically. Limitation: The implementation covers a particular family of obligationes, leaves some proof-system and knowledge assumptions under-specified, and identifies complexity questions for future work; it is not a full Thomistic *quaestio*. Scholastic overlap: This is the closest protocol-level analogue in the set because it directly models regulated opposition, constrained reply, and terminal evaluation, although it lacks the named *sed contra*, *respondeo*, and reply-to-objection stages. Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10503-012-9266-7

## 2. Maudet, Parsons & Rahwan (2007), “Argumentation in Multi-Agent Systems: Context and Recent Developments”
Structure: This survey organizes computational argumentation around conflicting arguments, their interaction, and dialogue protocols across reasoning and communication tasks. Method: It reviews argumentation-based belief revision, deliberation, planning, persuasion, inquiry, negotiation, and dialogue games, including locutions and commencement, commitment, combination, and termination rules. Finding: The survey argues that argumentation supplies a principled way for agents to reconcile information and reach consistent conclusions, while argument schemes and critical questions provide structured ways to challenge claims. Limitation: It is a selective, explicitly “idiosyncratic” survey rather than a single implemented system, and its general dialogue-game treatment does not prescribe a scholastic answer schema. Scholastic overlap: Its rule-governed proponent/opponent interaction and critical-question machinery map naturally onto objection generation and reply control, while its warning against overly rigid proof dialogues supports flexible handling of alternative scholastic replies. Source: https://web.mit.edu/irahwan/www/docs/argmas2006survey.pdf

## 3. Castagna, Kökciyan, Sassoon, Parsons & Sklar (2024), “Computational Argumentation-based Chatbots: a Survey”
Structure: The paper classifies argumentation-based chatbots by four functions—argument extraction, reply structuring, argumentation-based reasoning, and reply delivery—and surveys dialogue types such as inquiry, persuasion, deliberation, negotiation, verification, and education. Method: The authors define computational-argumentation chatbot criteria, review systems against abstract and structured argumentation frameworks, argument schemes, critical questions, semantics, and evaluation practices, then synthesize benefits and open problems for generative-LLM chatbots. Finding: Computational argumentation offers a route to more coherent, inspectable, and explainable chatbot responses, but the literature contains relatively few mature systems and evaluations are uneven. Limitation: The survey depends on heterogeneous prior systems, many of which are domain-limited, knowledge-base dependent, or unable to support broad conversational interaction; it does not itself implement or validate a complete disputation engine. Scholastic overlap: Its argument graphs, typed attacks, critical questions, and protocol-based reply structures provide a broad substrate for a machine-readable objection-to-reply pipeline, but the scholastic stages and distinction between objection types remain to be specified. Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2401.03454v1

## 4. Bolton, Calderwood, Christensen, Kafrouni & Drori (2020), “High Quality Real-Time Structured Debate Generation”
Structure: The system represents a debate as a tree of arguments and debate paths, with stance-based branching and a high-level grammar for multi-turn generation. Method: The authors train and evaluate a framework over a corpus of tree-structured debates annotated with argument metadata; generation is designed to be embedding-model agnostic and is assessed using competitive-debate criteria for style, content, and strategy. Finding: The system produces complex debates in real time at quality judged close to human performance on those metrics, and releases data, models, and code for reproducibility. Limitation: The evaluation does not test whether a debate is complete or covers the most important points, and generated responses can contain unknown-token artifacts that may bias human judgments; the structure is stance-oriented rather than semantically typed. Scholastic overlap: Debate trees and paths are a strong structural analogue for a root question with nested objections and replies, but the system lacks explicit *sed contra*, *respondeo*, role semantics, and per-objection resolution. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.00209

## 5. Kargupta, Agarwal, August & Han (2025), “Tree-of-Debate: Multi-Persona Debate Trees Elicit Critical Thinking for Scientific Comparative Analysis”
Structure: Tree-of-Debate turns papers into specialized LLM personas and expands a dynamically branching debate tree whose nodes compare contributions, similarities, and differences before synthesis. Method: The authors combine segment-level retrieval, persona construction, self-deliberation, adaptive debate-depth expansion, and debate synthesis, then compare against baselines on scientific comparative-analysis datasets using domain-expert and human evaluations. Finding: Structured debate substantially improves contextualization—reported at an average 95.21% versus 75.57% for the strongest baseline—and supports richer comparative summaries, though gains vary by domain and depth. Limitation: Factuality can decline in domains with less representation in model pretraining, and the approach remains dependent on LLM retrieval, persona quality, and debate-depth choices; its target is literature comparison rather than proposition-level truth adjudication. Scholastic overlap: Specialized disputant roles and dynamic branching resemble assigned participants and expanding objection/counter-objection trees, but the system does not impose a canonical *quaestio* protocol or a principled *respondeo*. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1422/ ; https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14767v2

## 6. Vasileiou, Kumar, Yeoh, Son & Toni (2024), “Dialectical Reconciliation via Structured Argumentative Dialogues”
Structure: DR-Arg models an explainer agent and an explainee whose knowledge differs, using structured arguments and counterarguments in a dialogue that terminates when the explainee understands the relevant decision model. Method: The paper gives operational semantics and theoretical guarantees for termination and success, then evaluates the framework through computational experiments and human-subject studies. Finding: Argumentative dialogue is presented as a viable way to reconcile knowledge discrepancies and improve human understanding of AI decisions, extending model-reconciliation approaches beyond single-shot explanations and assumed human models. Limitation: The framework is aimed at explainability and convergence on an AI decision, not at open-ended truth-seeking or historical scholastic disputation; its understanding metric and domain assumptions constrain generalization. Scholastic overlap: It is close to an explanatory *respondeo*: the explainer gives reasons, addresses counterarguments, and seeks a satisfactory reconciliation, but it does not explicitly separate objections, *sed contra*, main determination, and individual replies. Source: https://proceedings.kr.org/2024/73/kr2024-0073-vasileiou-et-al.pdf

## 7. Faugier, Armetta, Bonifati & Yun (2024), “Assisted Debate Builder with Large Language Models”
Structure: ADBL2 represents debates as bipolar argumentation trees whose edges encode support or attack, while preserving an editable, inspectable tree artifact. Method: The authors build an open-source debate-builder interface, use relation-based argument mining to verify existing relations and propose new arguments, and fine-tune a Mistral-7B model for relation classification across domains. Finding: The reported fine-tuned model reaches 90.59% overall F1 and is described as outperforming prior approaches, making it useful for semi-automated construction and correction of debate graphs. Limitation: The paper is a demonstration and relation-mining system rather than a complete dialogue manager; support/attack labels do not by themselves capture distinctions such as undercutting, qualification, distinction, or reply-to-reply, and performance depends on the available datasets and domains. Scholastic overlap: ADBL2 supplies the graph-construction layer needed to turn textual objections and replies into linked, reviewable structures, but it does not decide the scholastic role or discharge status of a move. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13015

## 8. Du, Li, Torralba, Tenenbaum & Mordatch (2023), “Improving Factuality and Reasoning in Language Models through Multiagent Debate”
Structure: Multiple instances of a language model independently propose answers and reasoning, debate them over several rounds, and converge on a common final answer. Method: The authors apply a shared debate procedure and prompts to black-box models across mathematical, strategic, and factuality-oriented tasks, comparing multiagent debate with single-model baselines. Finding: The paper reports improved mathematical and strategic reasoning and better factual validity, including fewer fallacious answers and hallucinations. Limitation: The protocol is primarily prompt-defined, and the work does not provide explicit argument types, provenance links, formal attack semantics, or a proof that the final consensus correctly resolves every objection; “debate” can therefore function as persuasive aggregation rather than inspectable disputation. Scholastic overlap: Its rounds of opposition followed by synthesis resemble objection, response, and judgment procedurally, but it lacks a stable question-centered schema and a reasoned distinction between main answer and replies. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325

## 9. Khan et al. (2024), “Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers”
Structure: Two stronger LLM debaters argue for opposing answers while a weaker model or human judge selects between them. Method: The study evaluates debate as a way for non-experts to assess expert-level answers and additionally optimizes debaters for persuasiveness without direct ground-truth labels. Finding: Debate improves answer selection, with the abstract reporting 76% accuracy for non-expert models and 88% for humans versus naive baselines of 48% and 60%; unsupervised persuasion optimization further improves truth identification. Limitation: The setup fixes two opposing sides and delegates resolution to a judge, so it does not require the Respondent to integrate objections into a principled determination; performance also depends on judge competence and persuasive asymmetries. Scholastic overlap: The paper operationalizes adversarial objection as a truth-seeking aid before judgment, but its winner-selection format is only a procedural near-analogue to disputation and lacks formal replies, distinctions, and authority handling. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06782

## 10. Hadoux & Hunter (2017), “Strategic Sequences of Arguments for Persuasion Using Decision Trees”
Structure: A persuasion dialogue is represented as a decision tree containing possible moves, chance or uncertainty, belief updates, and terminal valuations. Method: The authors transform persuasion into a decision problem, define update and valuation functions, compare established decision rules for selecting argument sequences, and analyze their behavior under uncertainty about the persuadee’s model. Finding: The framework shows how decision rules can prioritize moves strategically; in the reported experiments, MaxiMax performs best when the persuadee model is accurate, while rule performance changes as model error grows. Limitation: The objective is persuasive utility rather than correctness or principled discharge of objections, and practical use requires a suitable valuation function, update function, and model of the interlocutor. Scholastic overlap: It offers a planning mechanism for choosing which objection, critical question, or reply to present next, but its optimization target must be replaced or constrained by scholastic relevance and argumentative validity. Source: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10673/10532

## 11. Gorur, Rago & Toni (2024), “Can Large Language Models perform Relation-based Argument Mining?”
Structure: The paper treats relation-based argument mining as classification of relations among argument components, principally support and attack, as a prerequisite for building argument structures. Method: It evaluates prompted general-purpose and quantized open-source LLMs on ten datasets and compares them with a RoBERTa baseline. Finding: The study reports that Llama 70B-4bit and Mixtral 8x7B-4bit surpass the RoBERTa baseline, with Llama 70B-4bit performing better at the cost of slower inference and greater GPU requirements. Limitation: The main experiments use a binary support/attack task even though practical applications often require a ternary no-relation label; datasets are English-only, models are constrained by hardware, and the method uses prompting rather than fine-tuning. Scholastic overlap: This is enabling infrastructure for a disputation parser because a system must identify whether a passage supports, attacks, or relates to another, but the coarse relation inventory does not yet distinguish objection, rebuttal, undercutter, qualification, or reply. Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2402.11243

## 12. Peldszus & Stede (2019), “Determining Relative Argument Specificity and Stance for Complex Argumentative Structures”
Structure: The paper studies manually curated argument trees for 741 controversial topics, with 95,312 claims and generally two-to-six levels of depth, focusing on stance and relative specificity within complex structures. Method: It compares feature-based logistic regression, bag-of-words models, and fine-tuned BERT under controls for path distance, parent-child relation, and stance similarity. Finding: Claim length is strongly predictive of specificity; fine-tuned BERT performs best overall, while stance is easier for parent-child pairs and specificity becomes harder when claims are linguistically similar or evaluated under tighter controls. Limitation: The dataset and task address classification rather than dialogue generation or reply validation, and the authors leave integration into coherent argument generation for future work. Scholastic overlap: Specificity and stance modeling can help a scholastic system determine whether an objection targets the whole conclusion, a particular premise, or a narrower inference, supporting typed replies and distinctions rather than flat pro/con labeling. Source: https://aclanthology.org/P19-1456.pdf
