# Subtopic 02: Hallucination in Logical/Deductive Reasoning

This set contains paragraph summaries for the 11 retained primary studies identified in `agents/02_logic_hallucination.md`. Each paragraph states the study’s contribution, principal finding, limitation, and relevance to the project.

## 1. Xu et al. (2023), “Are Large Language Models Really Good Logical Reasoners? A Comprehensive Evaluation and Beyond”

Xu et al. evaluate seven LLMs across 15 datasets covering deductive, inductive, abductive, and mixed-form reasoning. Rather than treating answer accuracy as sufficient, they assess answers and explanations, classify logical flaws in evidence selection and reasoning, and propose a six-dimensional evaluation scheme that includes correctness, rigor, self-awareness, activity, orientation, and “no hallucination.” The study’s central contribution is therefore methodological: it makes unsupported or flawed reasoning an explicit evaluation dimension alongside final-answer correctness. Its limitation for this project is that the abstract-level benchmark description does not establish a reusable proof-span representation, a dedicated hallucination mechanism model, or coverage of the project’s cross-tradition argument forms. It nevertheless provides a strong precedent for scoring intermediate explanations and unsupported claims separately from final labels, supporting a deductive skeleton in which each generated edge can be checked rather than relying on answer accuracy alone. [Xu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09841)

## 2. Yuan et al. (2024), “Whispers that Shake Foundations: Analyzing and Mitigating False Premise Hallucinations in Large Language Models”

Yuan et al. directly study false-premise hallucination, defined as generating hallucinated text when confronted with a false-premise question. Their analysis identifies a small subset of attention heads—“false premise heads”—that disrupt knowledge extraction, and they introduce FAITH, an inference-time method that constrains those heads. The paper reports that constraining approximately 1% of attention heads yields nearly a 20% performance improvement in extensive experiments. The limitation is that the mechanism is an attention-head intervention rather than a general proof-structure or argument-scheme representation, and the task setting centers on false-premise questions rather than the full range of deductive forms. For this project, the paper is the closest direct precedent for placing premise validity before downstream generation: premise spans should be explicitly represented, checked for contradiction or falsity, and prevented from silently becoming inputs to later inference. [Yuan et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.155/)

## 3. Varshney et al. (2025), “Investigating and Addressing Hallucinations of LLMs in Tasks Involving Negation”

Varshney et al. examine hallucination when models must process negation, an operator central to logical reasoning and inference. They evaluate four tasks—false-premise completion, constrained fact generation, multiple-choice question answering, and fact generation with negation—and report considerable hallucination by open-source models including LLaMA-2-chat, Vicuna, and Orca-2 across these settings; they also test mitigation strategies. The study’s limitation is that it is a workshop paper focused mainly on factual-generation and QA tasks, so it does not establish behavior across complete formal deduction systems or diverse argument schemes. Its relevance is direct: polarity and negation cannot be treated as incidental wording during context compression or proof extraction. The project should preserve explicit positive/negative markers, attach them to premise and conclusion spans, and validate that a downstream inference has not dropped or reversed the negation that licensed the original claim. [Varshney et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.trustnlp-main.37/)

## 4. McKenna et al. (2023), “Sources of Hallucination by Large Language Models on Inference Tasks”

McKenna et al. use controlled natural-language-inference experiments with LLaMA, GPT-3.5, and PaLM families to identify pretraining biases that produce hallucination-like false entailments. They show that models can label a sample as entailed regardless of its premise when the hypothesis is attested in training data, with entities functioning as indices into memorized material; they also identify a corpus-level predicate-frequency bias in which relative frequency predicts false entailment behavior. Models perform worse on test samples that do not conform to these biases, providing useful controls for evaluation. NLI is only a proxy for proof and does not capture long multi-step deduction or every form of invalid derivation, so the paper’s mechanism account should not be generalized into a complete reasoning theory. For the project, the result demonstrates that local premise–hypothesis relations can be overridden by memorization and distributional cues, making premise-conditioned entailment and adversarially controlled distractors necessary evaluation targets. [McKenna et al. 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.182/)

## 5. Hu et al. (2023), “Won’t Get Fooled Again: Answering Questions with False Premises”

Hu et al. introduce FalseQA, a dataset of 2,365 human-written false-premise questions with explanations of the false premise and revised true-premise questions. They find that pretrained language models are easily deceived by tricky questions even though they often possess the knowledge needed to rebut them; fine-tuning on moderate amounts of data, such as 256 examples, improves false-premise discrimination, and models can generate reasonable rebuttal explanations. The study is limited by its question-answering focus, its emphasis on premise correction rather than general derivation, and its dependence on PLM-era supervised fine-tuning conditions. Its project relevance lies in separating knowledge availability from premise-validation behavior: a model may know the relevant fact but still answer inside an invalid premise. A proof-relevant context layer should therefore preserve the correction or rebuttal signal and give the model an explicit route to reject or repair an invalid premise before applying rules. [Hu et al. 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.309/)

## 6. Zhu et al. (2025), “KG-FPQ: Evaluating Factuality Hallucination in LLMs with Knowledge Graph-based False Premise Questions”

Zhu et al. propose an automated pipeline for generating false-premise questions from knowledge graphs. The method modifies true knowledge-graph triples to create false premises, uses GPT-based generation to produce semantically rich questions, and yields KG-FPQ, a benchmark of approximately 178,000 questions spanning three domains, six confusability levels, and two task formats. The paper’s main contribution is scalable, controlled evaluation of factuality hallucination under structured premise corruption, rather than a new formal proof calculus. Its limitation is that knowledge-graph triples are narrower and more atomic than natural-language arguments; the benchmark therefore does not directly measure multi-step proof validity, defeasible reasoning, or cross-tradition argument forms. For this project, the triple-corruption design is a useful template for generating controlled counterfactual and contradiction fixtures: perturb one premise or relation while holding the rest of a reasoning chain fixed, then test whether the model preserves provenance and refuses the unsupported deduction. [Zhu et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.698/)

## 7. Li et al. (2025), “Don’t Take the Premise for Granted: Evaluating the Premise Critique Ability of Large Language Models”

Li et al. define premise critique ability as the capacity to proactively identify and articulate errors in input premises and introduce PCBench, which combines four error types, three difficulty levels, and multifaceted evaluation. Across 15 representative LLMs, they report that most models rely heavily on explicit prompts and show limited autonomous critique; direct contradictions are easier than complex or procedural errors; reasoning ability does not consistently predict premise-critique ability; and flawed premises cause reasoning models to overthink and produce substantially longer responses. The abstract does not specify which formal logics dominate the benchmark, and “overthinking” is a reliability and efficiency symptom rather than a complete hallucination taxonomy. The project should nevertheless treat premise critique as a separate stage and label, not as an implicit consequence of general reasoning strength. This supports a pipeline that validates input premises before expansion and records whether a failure arose from accepting, repairing, or rejecting the premise. [Li et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.44/)

## 8. Hoppe, Ilievski, and Kalo (2025), “Investigating the Robustness of Deductive Reasoning with Large Language Models”

Hoppe, Ilievski, and Kalo study whether informal and autoformalization-based LLM deductive methods remain reliable under perturbation. They construct seven perturbed datasets from two families of interventions—adversarial noise and counterfactual statements—and organize reasoners by reasoning format, formalization syntax, and error-recovery feedback. The abstract reports that adversarial noise particularly affects autoformalization, counterfactual statements influence all approaches, and detailed feedback reduces syntax errors without improving overall accuracy, indicating limited self-correction. The study measures robustness and deduction accuracy rather than labeling every invalid inference as hallucination, and it does not provide a cross-tradition span classifier or a complete taxonomy of derivation failures. Its project relevance is strong: counterfactual and irrelevant spans should be treated as distinct from proof-relevant context, and evaluations should test distractor insertion, polarity changes, and order/dependency preservation rather than only clean instances. [Hoppe et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04352)

## 9. Mi et al. (2026), “Logical Consistency as a Bridge: Improving LLM Hallucination Detection via Label Constraint Modeling between Responses and Self-Judgments”

Mi et al. propose LaaB, a hallucination detector that connects implicit neural uncertainty features with explicit symbolic or verbalized self-judgments through logical label constraints. The framework uses a meta-judgment process to map symbolic labels into feature space and aligns the two views through mutual learning; experiments on four public datasets, four LLMs, and eight baselines report that LaaB outperforms the comparison systems. The limitation is conceptual as well as empirical: logical consistency functions here as a detection signal linking response and self-judgment labels, not necessarily as a formal proof checker or argument-form classifier, and exact benchmark-level error taxonomies require the full paper for deeper analysis. The study is nevertheless a direct precedent for using symbolic consistency after generation or retrieval. It supports adding a post-generation validation layer that checks whether the response, its stated judgment, and the retained context agree under explicit constraints. [Mi et al. 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.286/)

## 10. Tyen et al. (2024), “LLMs Cannot Find Reasoning Errors, But Can Correct Them Given the Error Location”

Tyen et al. separate logical-error localization from error correction across five reasoning tasks using BIG-Bench Mistake, a dataset of LLM-generated logical mistakes. They find that state-of-the-art LLMs generally struggle to locate even simple and objective reasoning errors, while correction improves when the error location is supplied; they further show that a small classifier trained on out-of-domain data can locate mistakes more effectively than prompting a large model. The benchmark’s mistakes are intentionally unambiguous and therefore may not capture the ambiguity of commonsense or philosophical reasoning, and successful correction after localization does not establish faithful internal reasoning. The project implication is architectural: the generation model should not be assumed to reliably critique its own deductive skeleton. Separate span-level localization, verifier, or classifier components should identify the failed edge or premise before asking a model to repair the chain. [Tyen et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516)

## 11. Li and Ng (2025), “Reasoning Models Hallucinate More: Factuality-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Large Reasoning Models”

Li and Ng study hallucination in long reasoning traces and report that outcome-based reinforcement learning can increase hallucination, with many errors originating in incorrect intermediate reasoning even when final-answer evaluation alone can obscure the problem. Their analysis attributes the effect to sparse outcome rewards and develops Factuality-aware Step-wise Policy Optimization (FSPO), which adds step-wise factuality verification to token-level advantages; the paper also reports that incorrect reasoning steps are the dominant cause in a manual analysis of cases where a reasoning model failed while a baseline succeeded. The limitation is that factuality is broader than formal validity: evidence-based verification may miss a formally invalid but factually plausible derivation, and the experiments center on mathematical and QA-style benchmarks rather than the project’s cross-tradition logic forms. The project should nonetheless score and provenance-track every intermediate proof step, not just the final answer, because a correct endpoint can coexist with an unsupported chain. [Li and Ng 2025](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/ddd50f29fa472095515fa0df31749e6c-Paper-Conference.pdf)

## Scope note

The retained set is a targeted subtopic review rather than an exhaustive bibliography. The inspected papers do not provide a single cross-tradition span-classification pipeline covering categorical syllogism, modus ponens/tollens, abduction, analogy, qiyas, and Nyāya inference while also constructing compact proof-relevant context for downstream generation. That gap is a project hypothesis, not a proof that no such paper exists.
