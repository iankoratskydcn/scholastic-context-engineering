# Subtopic 11 — Logical Fallacy Detection with LLMs

## Verified paper summaries

**1. Jin et al. (2022), “Logical Fallacy Detection.”** Jin et al. introduce the LOGIC dataset and the LOGICCLIMATE challenge set for detecting logical fallacies in general text and climate-change claims. Their central methodological claim is that fallacy detection requires modeling an argument’s underlying logical structure rather than treating the task as ordinary sentence classification; a structure-aware classifier outperformed the strongest evaluated pretrained language model by 5.46 F1 on LOGIC and 4.51 F1 on LOGICCLIMATE. The main limitation is that the examples are relatively short and educational, while the challenge set is narrowly focused on climate claims, so the benchmark does not test long-range discourse, evidence retrieval, or faithful proof generation. For this project, the paper establishes the foundational design principle that context should be organized around premises, conclusions, and intended relations before an LLM is asked to identify a fallacy. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.532/) and the linked PDF.

**2. Alhindi, Chakrabarty, Musi, and Muresan (2022), “Multitask Instruction-based Prompting for Fallacy Recognition.”** Alhindi et al. treat heterogeneous fallacy-recognition corpora as related tasks and train a multitask T5 prompting system across differences in input format, genre, and label inventory. The approach recognizes 28 fallacy types across domains and examines the effects of model size, prompt formulation, annotation quality, and external knowledge. Its main limitation is that it is primarily supervised T5-based recognition of supplied text: it does not reconstruct missing premises, recover a discourse graph, or retrieve evidence that establishes why an inference fails. The project relevance is direct because the work shows that context representation and task instructions must normalize heterogeneous argument formats; its external-knowledge analysis also motivates typed context augmentation rather than a single undifferentiated prompt. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.560/) and the linked PDF.

**3. Sourati et al. (2023), “Robust and Explainable Identification of Logical Fallacies in Natural Language Arguments.”** Sourati et al. formalize fallacy identification as a three-stage pipeline—binary detection, coarse-grained classification, and fine-grained classification—and evaluate prototype-based reasoning, instance-based reasoning, knowledge injection, data augmentation, and curriculum learning. The study finds that performance varies substantially by task component and fallacy class, and that data augmentation and curriculum learning can improve robustness while case- and knowledge-based methods provide explainability mechanisms. The limitation is that the framework consolidates heterogeneous prior datasets and its explanations are not necessarily faithful reconstructions of the exact premise-to-conclusion failure; injected knowledge is not selected by an explicit argument-graph or evidence-retrieval policy. For this project, it supplies a useful evaluation decomposition and supports treating fallacy classification as specialized reasoning over examples, prototypes, and relevant background knowledge rather than as a single label prediction. **Verified source:** [Knowledge-Based Systems](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950705123001685), with the matching [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07425).

**4. Goffredo et al. (2023), “Argument-based Detection and Classification of Fallacies in Political Debates.”** Goffredo et al. extend the ElecDeb60To16 political-debate corpus with token-level fallacy spans, claims, premises, support and attack relations, and six fallacy categories, then compare Transformer architectures that combine text representations with argumentative and engineered features. The reported result is that adding non-text argumentative features improves fallacy detection and classification over text-only alternatives. The limitation is domain concentration in U.S. presidential debates and a small six-class inventory; the relations are used as largely static model features rather than dynamically selected context or retrieved proof evidence, and the method does not generate an auditable object explaining why an edge is invalid. The paper is highly relevant here because it makes premise/claim boundaries and support/attack edges explicit, providing a concrete schema for selecting the surrounding context that a proof-oriented fallacy detector should preserve. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.684/) and the linked PDF.

**5. Payandeh et al. (2024), “How Susceptible Are LLMs to Logical Fallacies?”** Payandeh et al. introduce LOGICOM, a multi-round persuader–debater benchmark in which an LLM must update or defend a position while being exposed either to logically valid reasoning or to fallacious arguments. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 changed their opinions through reasoning, but were erroneously convinced 41% and 69% more often, respectively, under fallacious than logical conditions; the authors also release more than 5,000 paired logical/fallacious arguments. The limitation is that the benchmark measures susceptibility and decision change rather than accurate localization, classification, or explanation of a fallacy, and its paired debate scenarios may not represent naturally occurring discourse or domain-specific evidence. Its project relevance is that it demonstrates why local sentence context is insufficient: fallacy effects depend on prior turns, debate roles, claims, and the persuader’s reasoning strategy, making multi-round discourse context an important evaluation condition. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.726/) and the linked PDF.

**6. Glockner et al. (2024), “MISSCI: Reconstructing Fallacies in Misrepresented Science.”** Glockner et al. introduce MISSCI, a dataset and argumentation-theoretic task in which a misinformation claim misrepresents a cited biomedical publication. The model must classify the implicit fallacy and verbalize the invalid reasoning that connects the publication’s relevant content to the inaccurate claim; GPT-4 performs promisingly in zero-shot experiments, but human evaluation confirms that the task remains difficult. The limitation is the biomedical setting and curated, simplified study context, which do not fully test general discourse-graph recovery, and generated explanations can be inconsistent with the predicted label. For this project, MISSCI is a particularly strong template: proof-relevant context consists of the accurate study premise, publication-specific qualifiers such as species or population, and the reconstructed bridge that turns that premise into an unsupported claim. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.240/) and the linked PDF.

**7. Lei and Huang (2024), “Boosting Logical Fallacy Reasoning in LLMs via Logical Structure Tree.”** Lei and Huang construct an unsupervised logical-structure tree from constituency structure and a taxonomy of connective words, representing relation connectives as internal nodes and textual arguments as leaves. They inject this structure into LLMs either as a textualized hard prompt or as a relation-aware soft embedding, and report improved precision and recall for both fallacy detection and classification on benchmark datasets. The limitation is that connective-driven parsing can miss implicit relations, pragmatic context, and nonlocal discourse dependencies, while the recovered tree is not guaranteed to be the correct argument graph. The project relevance is exceptionally direct: the tree provides an explicit intermediate representation for selecting the premises and connective relation that should enter a fallacy-classification prompt instead of passing the entire flat string to the model. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.730/) and the linked PDF.

**8. Glockner et al. (2025), “Grounding Fallacies Misrepresenting Scientific Publications in Evidence.”** Glockner et al. extend MISSCI into MISSCI PLUS by grounding fallacious reasoning in real passages from the misrepresented biomedical studies and adding passage retrieval, fallacy verbalization, and fact-checking evaluations. The work shows that retrieval models must identify passages that only appear to support a claim through fallacious reasoning, that LLMs must verbalize the bridge from evidence to misinformation, and that current fact-checking models struggle to use such passages; the passages can even mislead LLMs into accepting false claims. The limitation is the curated biomedical setting and the partial separation between retrieval, classification, and fact-checking, leaving an end-to-end policy for selecting a minimal proof set unresolved. For this project, it is the strongest existing analogue for evidence-aware context construction: retrieve the passage supporting the accurate premise, preserve the domain qualifiers, identify the unsupported bridge, and test the resulting explanation against the source evidence. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.491/) and the linked PDF.

**9. Li et al. (2024), “Reason from Fallacy: Enhancing Large Language Models’ Logical Reasoning through Logical Fallacy Understanding.”** Li et al. define logical-fallacy understanding through five tasks spanning WHAT, WHY, and HOW: identifying the fallacy, explaining the violated reasoning pattern, and testing whether that understanding improves broader logical reasoning. They construct the LFUD dataset with GPT-4 assistance and limited human effort, then report that the dataset can evaluate and fine-tune LLMs, with fine-tuned models showing improved logical-reasoning performance. The limitation is that the taxonomy and generated explanations are broader than an explicit context-retrieval problem, and better task performance does not guarantee that a generated rationale is a valid or faithful proof. The project relevance is the decomposition into typed explanation slots: a proof-oriented system can separately represent the fallacy label (WHAT), the violated relation or missing assumption (WHY), and the downstream reasoning consequence (HOW). **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-naacl.192.pdf) and the corresponding Findings of NAACL 2024 volume record.

**10. Jeong, Jang, and Park (2025), “Large Language Models Are Better Logical Fallacy Reasoners with Counterargument, Explanation, and Goal-Aware Prompt Formulation.”** Jeong et al. enrich fallacy-detection inputs with model-generated contextual queries about counterarguments, explanations, and goals, assess those queries for validity within the argument context, and rank them by confidence before classification. Across datasets from five domains covering 29 fallacy types, the method reports gains of up to 0.57 F1 in zero-shot settings and 0.45 F1 in fine-tuned settings in the official abstract. The limitation is that query confidence and ranking are model-internal heuristics, so an improvement in benchmark F1 does not establish that the elicited context is causally sufficient or that the explanation is faithful. The project relevance is direct: counterargument, goal, and explanation are useful typed context slots for distinguishing an invalid inference from a merely controversial claim and for exposing what evidence is missing. **Verified source:** [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.384/) and the linked PDF.

**11. Papadopulos, Alam, and Da San Martino (2026), “Beyond Logical Forms: LLM-Extracted Patterns for Fallacy Classification.”** Papadopulos et al. investigate whether abstract logical forms become more useful when combined with context-level linguistic cues. Their framework uses LLMs to inductively extract reusable patterns from fallacious examples and their explanations, then evaluates those patterns in zero-shot and one-shot settings across multiple models and datasets; the reported experiments show statistically significant gains over zero-shot baselines and cross-dataset generalization. The limitation is that this is very recent preprint/workshop-stage evidence, so independent replication, adversarial paraphrase testing, and robustness to demonstration artifacts remain open; extracted patterns may inherit errors or label-specific surface cues. For this project, the paper supports storing a fallacy signature in two layers—an abstract argument form plus contextual and rhetorical cues—rather than discarding surface context as irrelevant noise. **Verified source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26698); the arXiv record was available, while the workshop listing was used only as a publication lead.

**12. Xu et al. (2026), “Socrates or Smartypants: Testing Logic Reasoning Capabilities of Large Language Models with Logic Programming-Based Test Oracles.”** Xu et al. introduce SMARTYPAT-BENCH, a benchmark of 502 naturally expressed Reddit posts with fine-grained fallacy labels, and SMARTYPAT, a generation framework that uses Prolog rules as logic-programming test oracles before LLMs realize controlled fallacies in fluent natural language. The authors report that the generated fallacies are comparable in subtlety and quality to human-authored examples, while structured reasoning improves categorization but excessive reasoning steps can reduce detection accuracy. The limitation is that synthetic coverage depends on the rule inventory and the quality of LLM realization, and Reddit/English selection limits generalization; a Prolog oracle verifies the intended formal pattern but not all pragmatic or domain-specific aspects of a real fallacy. The project relevance is the clean separation of formal validity checking from linguistic classification: symbolic rules can validate the intended argument form, while naturally varied text supplies the contextual stress test. **Verified source:** [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/39021) and the official PDF link on that page.

**13. Dore, Damo, Cabrio, and Villata (2026), “Retrieving Relations, Detecting Fallacies: A RAG Approach to Political Debate Analysis.”** Dore et al. propose a retrieval-augmented method that uses support and attack relations in political debates to dynamically steer retrieval from a 15 GB political knowledge base, replacing purely static argumentative features with relation-guided evidence selection. On the ElecDeb60to20 benchmark, evaluated across 42 retrieval configurations and 14 models, the paper reports improvements up to 0.864 macro-F1 for fallacy detection and 0.725 for classification over non-retrieval baselines. The limitation is that it is a very recent arXiv preprint focused on English U.S. presidential debates and a domain-tailored knowledge base; the results therefore require replication outside that setting, and retrieval/classification success does not itself prove that the selected passages form a faithful minimal proof. This is the clearest recent lead for the project’s dynamic context-selection goal because it makes argument relations operational query controls for retrieving external evidence rather than merely adding them as classifier features. **Verified source:** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27471), published August 18, 2026; no DOI was verified.
