# Subtopic 16 — Computational history of logic and formalizing historical logics

## Paragraph summaries

**1. Aristotle, *Prior Analytics*.** Aristotle’s *Prior Analytics*, especially Books I–II, is the primary ancient source for categorical syllogistic as a theory of terms, premises, figures, moods, conversion, reduction, and demonstration. Its historical contribution is not merely a list of valid patterns: it presents a vocabulary and proof practice in which some syllogisms are “perfect,” others are derived by reduction, and demonstration is tied to necessary consequence. For this project, it should be treated as a source layer from which argument templates and deduction traces are extracted, with Greek wording, translation, quantifier scope, existential assumptions, modality, and textual uncertainty preserved. A formalizer must distinguish Aristotle’s own procedures from later predicate-logical recodings; success in a modern encoding establishes a property of that reconstruction, not proof that Aristotle endorsed the modern system. Source: [Perseus Digital Library](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0053).

**2. John Corcoran, “Aristotle’s Prior Analytics and the Theory of Syllogism” (1974).** Corcoran’s contribution is to present Aristotelian syllogistic as an internally structured deductive system rather than as an unfinished anticipation of modern predicate logic. The key historical/formal distinction is between a deductional reconstruction—where rules, derivations, and proof structure matter—and a semantic or model-theoretic reconstruction that may redescribe the system from outside. This supports a project architecture in which historical calculi retain their own primitives, derivation steps, and proof-theoretic behavior while remaining comparable to modern formalisms through explicit translations. Corcoran’s Aristotelian focus is a strength for the ancient module but also a boundary: medieval semantic theories, Arabic relational developments, Indian inference, and Mohist disputation require separate schemas rather than being treated as extensions of one syllogistic core. Source route: [WorldCat record for *Ancient Logic and Its Modern Interpretations*](https://search.worldcat.org/title/ancient-logic-and-its-modern-interpretations/oclc/1020378).

**3. Klaus Glashoff, “Aristotelian Syntax from a Computational–Combinatorial Point of View” (2005).** Glashoff provides the most direct computational precedent in this set. He formalizes Aristotle’s logical system through rule-based reduction relations on a defined basic set, applies structural tools such as the Newman lemma, reproduces Aristotle’s derivational method, and analyzes the resulting rule system with a computer program. Historically, the paper reinforces the view that Aristotelian syllogistic is a formal system in its own right, not merely a fragment awaiting translation into predicate logic; formally, it demonstrates how typed terms, reduction rules, normalization behavior, and generated derivation traces can be made executable. The project can directly adapt this pattern for a provenance-preserving Aristotelian module, while keeping syntax, semantics, and later interpretive additions separate. Source: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/logcom/article-abstract/15/6/949/1086849) and [author PDF](https://glashoff.eu/Texte/reduction2.pdf).

**4. Catarina Dutilh Novaes, *Formalizing Medieval Logical Theories: Suppositio, Consequentiae and Obligationes* (2007).** Dutilh Novaes formalizes three major medieval areas—supposition theory, Buridan’s theory of consequence, and obligationes—and then examines what formalization itself does to historical material. The historical contribution is to show that medieval logic includes semantic-pragmatic and dialogical practices that cannot be reduced to a small set of Aristotelian syllogistic moods; the formal contribution is an “algorithmic hermeneutics” that makes interpretive choices explicit rather than pretending that formal notation is neutral transcription. For this project, the book supplies a methodology and several candidate executable schemas: context-sensitive term interpretation, consequence relations, stipulated cases, commitment and retraction rules, and logical games. Its Latin-medieval focus means that Arabic, Indian, and Mohist materials should be formalized with their own technical vocabularies and ontological commitments. Source: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4020-5853-0).

**5. Alexander Broadie, *Introduction to Medieval Logic*, 2nd ed. (1993).** Broadie’s synthesis maps the breadth of late medieval logical analysis through figures including Walter Burley, William Ockham, John Buridan, Albert of Saxony, and Paul of Venice. It covers truth, categorical and molecular propositions, validity conditions, supposition—including intentional contexts—and medieval syllogistics, thereby documenting how far medieval logicians developed beyond the Aristotelian core. Its historical value for the project is curatorial: it identifies families of phenomena that a corpus must not collapse into “syllogism,” while its formal relevance lies in the distinctions that need annotation before executable rules can be written. Because it is a scholarly overview rather than a machine-readable specification, implementation would still require edition-level alignment, terminology normalization, competing analyses, and explicit treatment of context-sensitive reference. Source: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/6319).

**6. Catarina Dutilh Novaes and Stephen Read (eds.), *The Cambridge Companion to Medieval Logic* (2016).** This companion presents medieval logic as a connected field spanning ancient inheritance, Arabic logic before and after Avicenna, Latin traditions, and thematic developments in supposition, propositions, sophisms, syllogism, consequence, modality, and obligationes. Its historical contribution is corrective: it resists narrow national or chronological boundaries and foregrounds transmission, adaptation, and internal innovation across Greek, Syriac/Arabic, and Latin contexts. For the project, it motivates a provenance graph in which a formal rule can be linked to a source passage, commentator, translation lineage, and later adaptation, while structural similarity is kept distinct from documented historical transmission. The volume does not itself establish a common annotation language, so the missing computational layer is a shared alignment and uncertainty model rather than a universal medieval calculus. Source: [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-medieval-logic/frontmatter/90B806867E65585809C37DBE09390BC7).

**7. Paolo Mancosu and Massimo Mugnai, *Syllogistic Logic and Mathematical Proof* (2023).** Mancosu and Mugnai trace the long debate over whether syllogistic can capture mathematical proof, from Aristotle through medieval authors and later figures such as Kant and De Morgan. The relevant historical/formal contribution is their treatment of medieval extensions of syllogistic, including oblique terms, relational sentences, and expository syllogism, within a broader account of the relationship between syllogistic reasoning and mathematical practice. For this project, the book supplies concrete rule families that should be represented separately from categorical syllogism: relational and oblique propositions, expository reasoning, and historically situated claims about what counts as proof. It also warns against evaluating historical systems solely by modern adequacy criteria; the computational task is to model the resources and limits of each reconstruction, not to declare one tradition universally sufficient. Source: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/46132).

**8. Khaled El-Rouayheb, *Relational Syllogisms and the History of Arabic Logic, 900–1900* (2010).** El-Rouayheb reconstructs a millennium of Arabic logical work on relational and “unfamiliar” syllogisms, drawing on manuscripts and rare books across regions and periods from the tenth century through the nineteenth. Its historical contribution is substantial: it challenges the idea that Islamic logic merely preserved Greek logic or stagnated after the medieval period, showing sustained debate and original development, including Ottoman analyses of relational inference as a distinct kind of syllogism. For the project, this is the central Arabic module and a strong reason to represent relation-sensitive inference as historically primary rather than as an exception to Aristotelian logic. A computational implementation should preserve Arabic technical terms, manuscript and translation provenance, chronology, and the distinction between inherited Aristotelian forms and later developments; the book itself is historical analysis, not an executable calculus. Source: [RMIT bibliographic record](https://researchrepository.rmit.edu.au/discovery/fulldisplay?context=L&docid=alma9921478072501341&vid=61RMIT_INST%3ARMITU).

**9. Wilfrid Hodges, “Avicenna’s Logics with Sentence-Type Terms: 1. Wholly *muttaṣil* Syllogisms” (published online 2024; issue 2025).** Hodges analyzes an early Avicennian work, the *Short Epitome in Logic*, in which hypothetical logic is built from Aristotelian-looking syllogistic patterns but uses sentences rather than nouns as terms. The historical contribution is to identify a neglected change in the term domain and to show how Avicenna’s system anticipates ideas later associated with adverbs of quantification and Boole’s secondary propositions. Formally, it is a valuable test of typed historical representation: identical surface labels such as “syllogism” can conceal different syntactic categories, quantificational behavior, and semantics. The project should therefore encode the term ontology and sentence-type structure explicitly, rather than importing a noun-term Aristotelian schema; the article covers one Avicennian work and one family of syllogisms, so it cannot stand for Islamic logic as a whole. Source: [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/logcom/article-abstract/35/7/exae032/7721282).

**10. Bimal Krishna Matilal, *The Character of Logic in India* (1998).** Matilal presents Indian logic as a diverse philosophical and linguistic tradition centered not only on formal deduction but also on debate, proof, inference, epistemic sources, defeaters, and the relation between language and reality. The Nyāya five-member inference—thesis, reason, example, application, and conclusion—is tied to *vyāpti* (pervasion or invariable concomitance), while the book also treats Buddhist, Jain, and later Navya-Nyāya developments. Its historical contribution is therefore anti-reductive: Indian logical practices should not be measured only by whether they instantiate Aristotelian or Fregean forms. For this project, Matilal supports a Nyāya argument record that stores warrant, example, application, epistemic status, and defeater conditions alongside the conclusion, while retaining Sanskrit technical terms and distinguishing modern analytic paraphrase from historical doctrine. Source: [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Character_of_Logic_in_India.html?hl=en&id=NzZRu12ngLAC&output=html_text).

**11. Jonardon Ganeri (ed.), *Indian Logic: A Reader* (2001).** Ganeri’s reader assembles landmark primary and secondary materials from the modern study of Indian logic, including work on Nyāya, the Indian syllogism, research methodology, the concept of *pakṣa*, Navya-Nyāya inference, and the history of interpretation. Its historical contribution is historiographical as well as substantive: it exposes how modern scholarship has framed Indian reasoning, including the danger of treating “Indian logic” as a single homogeneous system or judging it only by comparison with Western logic. For the project, the reader is useful as a corpus-construction guide: passages need tradition labels, source dates, original-language text, translation alternatives, and metadata about scholarly disagreement. It does not provide a canonical formal calculus, so computational work should preserve competing readings and distinguish Nyāya, Buddhist, Jain, and Navya-Nyāya materials rather than normalizing them into one Indian schema. Source: [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Indian-Logic-A-Reader/Ganeri/p/book/9780203037119).

**12. Mrityunjoy Panday and Sudipta Ghosh, “Cubical Type Theoretic Navya-Nyāya” (2026).** Panday and Ghosh propose a formalization of Navya-Nyāya’s technical language in CCHM De Morgan cubical type theory. The paper’s formal contribution is unusually ambitious: it encodes relation, delimitor (*avacchedaka*), absence (*abhāva*), pervasion (*vyāpti*), identity, higher relations, paryāpti, and qualifier–qualificand structure; it also reports internal signature theorems, metatheoretic results, worked encodings of *Tattvacintāmaṇi* passages, and a Cubical Agda implementation sketch. Historically, its importance is methodological because it treats dependent delimitation, typed absence, non-extensional identity, and unbounded relational depth as load-bearing features rather than noise to be flattened into first-order predicates. The project can use it as the clearest precedent for a typed relational intermediate representation, while treating its claims as frontier preprint evidence requiring independent validation against Sanskrit commentaries and broader Nyāya history. Source: [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12548).

**13. A. C. Graham, *Later Mohist Logic, Ethics and Science* (1978).** Graham’s translation and analysis reconstructs the Later Mohist Canons and related writings as a rare surviving body of ancient Chinese work on logic, ethics, optics, mechanics, geometry, names, descriptions, and methods of disputation. Its historical contribution is to make a technically rich but textually difficult tradition available for systematic study while emphasizing that Mohist reasoning is not simply an uncompleted Aristotelian syllogistic. For this project, the book suggests a different computational vocabulary: lexical distinctions, definitions, classification, analogy, counterexample, disputational moves, and empirical or technical reasoning should be modeled as a family of operations linked to original Chinese text, translation choices, emendations, and commentary. The major limitation is philological instability: terse Canons and damaged transmission make Graham’s reconstruction an interpretive layer that must not be silently encoded as uncontested primary meaning. Source route: [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/later-mohist-logic-ethics-and-science/oclc/4495009) and [bibliographic/contents record](https://obnb.uk/p6734657-later-mohist-logic-ethics-and-science).

**14. Catarina Dutilh Novaes, *Formal Languages in Logic: A Philosophical and Cognitive Analysis* (2012).** Dutilh Novaes broadens the notion of formal language beyond mathematical symbol systems and analyzes the historical, philosophical, and cognitive roles that formalisms play in reasoning. Its contribution to the history of logic is a methodological guardrail: formalization is an intervention that makes some distinctions explicit, changes cognitive access to inferential structure, and can suppress or distort other distinctions; therefore, historical practices need not use modern formal languages to be logically sophisticated. For this project, the book supports evaluating an encoding by what it preserves, exposes, and loses, and by documenting the intended level of abstraction rather than claiming tradition-neutrality. It does not prescribe a data model, theorem prover, or cross-tradition benchmark, so its role is to govern the design of the layered representation: source text, translation, tradition-specific schema, executable encoding, proof trace, and disagreement record. Source: [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/formal-languages-in-logic/7D1DD805F7B3C70A0C32F2F6A66BE3DD).

## Synthesis for the project

Together, these sources support a provenance-preserving argument record rather than a universal historical logic. Each record should retain source passage and edition, language and translation, tradition and date range, premises and claims, inferential operation, semantic commitments, defeaters or exceptions, confidence, formal encoding, proof/check trace, and unresolved scholarly alternatives. The strongest shared computational object is a historically typed transition from linguistic material to conclusion; comparison should occur at multiple levels—surface structure, rule behavior, semantic commitments, and proof-theoretic properties—without treating successful modern verification as evidence that the historical author accepted the modern encoding.
