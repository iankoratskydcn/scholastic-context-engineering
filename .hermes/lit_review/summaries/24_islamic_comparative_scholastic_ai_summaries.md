# Subtopic 24 — Islamic, Comparative, and Scholastic AI/NLP: Paper Summaries

## 1. Hodges, “Ibn Sīnā on Analysis: 1. Proof Search. Or: Abstract State Machines as a Tool for History of Logic”
Hodges studies Avicenna’s discussion of analysis in *Qiyās* as a historical proof-search procedure for syllogisms, representing its states and transitions with an Abstract State Machine. The evidence is a close reading, translation, and formal reconstruction of the Arabic logical passage rather than a machine-learning experiment or benchmark. Its main limitation is scope: it formalizes one logical procedure and does not model the semantics, rhetoric, or social context of broader kalām or scholastic debate. For this project, it is the strongest direct precedent for converting a medieval Arabic reasoning procedure into an executable, inspectable computational representation, especially a target representation for premises, transformations, and conclusions. [1]

## 2. Hodges, “A Correctness Proof for al-Barakāt’s Logical Diagrams”
This article reconstructs Abū al-Barakāt’s twelfth-century diagrammatic method for finding conclusions of premise-pairs and identifying pairs with no conclusion, then gives a rigorous proof that the procedure is correct. The computational method is symbolic reconstruction and correctness proof, not NLP: the author reverse-engineers a procedure that the medieval source presents without instructions or proof. The evidence is the logical behavior of the reconstructed diagrams and the proof that the resulting outputs match the intended syllogistic consequences. The limitation is that the system remains a narrow formal treatment of syllogistic inference and does not address historical language processing, argument identification, or theological disagreement. It matters here as a model for translating Arabic logical artifacts into verifiable symbolic-AI components rather than treating them as opaque historical prose. [2]

## 3. Abdelwahab, Daghbouche, and Shannan, “The Algorithm of Islamic Jurisprudence (Fiqh) with Validation of an Entscheidungsproblem”
This paper proposes a formal account of *uṣūl al-fiqh* by expressing selected jurisprudential questions through mathematical logic, a logic-query language, and a generic algorithm intended to decide legal rulings. Its method is conceptual formalization using subsets of first-order logic, with discussion of completeness, decidability, complexity, and an Islamic-law Entscheidungsproblem. The evidence is the paper’s definitions, worked methodological distinctions, and proposed formal architecture, not a released implementation, corpus, or empirical evaluation. Its limitation is substantial: broad claims about complete algorithmic coverage and decidability are programmatic and should not be treated as validated performance results; it also concerns jurisprudence rather than kalām proper. It is relevant as an adjacent example of rule-based knowledge representation for Islamic scholastic reasoning and as a caution against confusing formal specification with demonstrated historical or legal competence. [3]

## 4. Abdelwahab et al., “Computational Extraction of Legal Causes via al-Ṣabr wa al-Taqsīm: A Set-Theoretic Formalization for Closed Fiqh Chapters”
This paper formalizes the classical uṣūl al-fiqh technique of *al-ṣabr wa al-taqsīm*—examination and division—as a set-theoretic procedure for extracting candidate legal causes within closed jurisprudential chapters. The computational method maps the traditional elimination and classification process into sets, predicates, and operations that can in principle support rule-based extraction. The evidence is the explicit mathematical reconstruction of the method and its application to legal-cause analysis, rather than a trained NLP model or benchmarked information-extraction system. Its limitation is the closed-domain and highly formal setting: results do not establish robustness on varied Arabic legal prose, contested interpretations, or theological argument. For this project, it demonstrates how a historically situated scholastic technique can become an executable intermediate layer between textual evidence and an argument graph, while confirming that current formalization work is concentrated in fiqh rather than comparative kalām. [4]

## 5. Maliszewski, “It Takes a Village to Write a Book: Mapping Anonymous Contributions in Stephen Langton’s *Quaestiones Theologiae*”
Maliszewski presents a registered-report protocol for analyzing collaborative production and editorial layers in Stephen Langton’s medieval Christian theological *quaestiones*, which derive from *reportationes* of oral teaching. The method combines handwritten-text recognition, automated transcription alignment, stylometry based on frequent words, part-of-speech sequences, pseudo-affixes, and unsupervised clustering. The evidence includes exploratory principal-component analyses separating Langton’s corpus from other scholastic collections and revealing internal stylistic diversity, while the proposed study compares manually edited with HTR-extracted data. Its chief limitation is that short *quaestiones* provide weak stylometric samples and the work primarily identifies authorship and transmission layers, not argumentative structure or doctrinal validity. It is directly useful here as a Christian scholastic preprocessing and corpus-method precedent: reliable modeling of disputation requires first separating reportators, editors, witnesses, and textual layers. [5][6]

## 6. Maliszewski, published Cambridge version of “It Takes a Village to Write a Book”
The Cambridge-published version confirms the same registered-report study and supplies the bibliographic and publication record for the Langton investigation: the corpus contains multiple versions of many *quaestiones*, and the proposed workflow compares stylometry on manually edited text with transformer-based HTR output. Its computational method remains function-word and other stylometric features, POS sequences, pseudo-affixes, clustering, and transcription alignment, while the evidence is the article’s exploratory PCA and corpus statistics rather than a completed argument-mining evaluation. The limitation is unchanged: short texts weaken authorial-style inference, and authorship or editorial-layer detection does not recover the semantic structure of scholastic objections and replies. The published record is relevant to this project as a verified, reusable computational-humanities protocol for corpus formation and transmission-aware preprocessing; it should be treated as the same study as the open preprint, not as independent corroboration. [6]

## 7. Lange et al., “Text Mining Islamic Law”
This article surveys and demonstrates the use of digital-humanities and text-mining methods in Islamic legal studies, including corpus construction, computational comparison, and analysis of Qur’anic and legal materials across schools of law. Its method is empirical text mining combined with historical and legal interpretation, using machine-readable corpora to test or refine claims in the secondary literature. The evidence is the article’s comparative computational analyses, which in some cases confirm scholarly claims and in others correct them. Its limitation is disciplinary and representational: text mining can reveal distributional and textual patterns, but it does not by itself recover the full force of legal reasoning, authority, intent, or dialectical exchange. The relation to this project is infrastructural and methodological: it shows that computational Islamic studies can be historically meaningful when corpus design and domain interpretation remain explicit, while underscoring the lack of mature argument-mining resources for kalām. [7]

## 8. OpenITI, Open Islamicate Texts Initiative
OpenITI is a machine-actionable corpus initiative for premodern Arabic and other Islamicate texts rather than a single research paper or AI model. Its computational contribution is corpus encoding, normalization, metadata, versioning, and machine-readable text organization designed to support search, quantitative analysis, authorship study, terminology extraction, and future NLP. The evidence is the initiative’s description of its corpus goals, scholarly data model, and coverage of the written Arabic and Islamicate heritage. Its limitations include uneven edition alignment, OCR and transcription noise, incomplete genre and author metadata, and the need for expert validation before using texts as gold-standard training data. For this project, OpenITI is the principal enabling infrastructure for assembling comparable corpora across kalām, falsafa, fiqh, Jewish-Arabic philosophy, and related traditions; it is a prerequisite resource, not evidence that comparative scholastic argument mining has already been solved. [8]

## 9. Yousef et al., “Enhancing State-of-the-Art NLP Models for Classical Arabic”
Yousef and colleagues develop Classical-Arabic NLP resources and fine-tune deep-learning models for named-entity recognition, person-relationship classification, toponym classification, onomastic-section detection, onomastic-element classification, and date recognition. The method uses a large OpenITI-derived subcorpus, active learning, expert annotation, iterative correction, and fine-tuning of Classical-Arabic transformer models. The evidence includes reported improvements such as fine-tuned NER F1 scores around 94% for persons and toponyms and an onomastic-boundary F1 around 88%, alongside a corpus of roughly 71 million tokens and hundreds of thousands of biographies. Limitations include the in-progress status, task-specific biography domain, sparse expert labels, weaker performance for underrepresented classes and toponym relations, and no direct modeling of premises or rebuttals. It supplies the language-processing layer needed before extracting authorities, participants, and textual relations from historical Islamic scholastic works. [9]

## 10. Hawwari, Diab, and Zaghouani, “A Pilot PropBank Annotation for Quranic Arabic”
This paper creates a pilot Quranic Arabic PropBank by assigning predicate-argument structures and semantic roles to the 50 most frequent verbs in the Quranic Arabic Dependency Treebank. The computational method combines dependency syntax, manually created verb frames, Arabic root-meaning references, double annotation, and adjudication; the pilot covers 7,227 verbal occurrences and is intended eventually to extend to all 1,466 Quranic verb types. The evidence is the feasibility of constructing frame files and the observed sense differences between Quranic Arabic and modern newswire Arabic, including greater ambiguity in the Quranic resource. Its limitation is coverage: the pilot annotates only 50 verbs and semantic roles are not equivalent to discourse-level argumentation, doctrinal inference, or intertextual authority. For this project, it is a foundational semantic layer for distinguishing linguistic predicate structure from later theological interpretation and for building Arabic resources that can feed argument-mining pipelines. [10]

## 11. Altammami and Atwell, “Challenging the Transformer-based Models with a Classical Arabic Dataset: Quran and Hadith”
This study evaluates monolingual, bilingual, and multilingual transformer models on semantic relatedness between Quran verses and Hadith teachings, using a deliberately difficult Classical-Arabic dataset assembled with expert consultation. The method trains or fine-tunes models on Quran–Quran relatedness data and tests generalization on Quran–Hadith pairs, reducing the risk that random splits merely measure memorization. The evidence is the dataset-construction methodology, cross-domain evaluation, and the reported finding that current models struggle with the complex semantics, orthography, rhetoric, and implicit meanings of Quranic and Hadith texts. Its limitation is task scope: binary semantic relatedness is not structured argument mining, and performance depends on expert pair construction rather than a complete model of religious reasoning. It provides a useful evaluation warning for this project: strong results on modern Arabic or in-domain splits do not establish competence with historically situated scholastic language or reasoning. [11]

## 12. Alnefaie, Atwell, and Alsalka, “HAQA and QUQA: Constructing Two Arabic Question-Answering Corpora for the Quran and Hadith”
This paper constructs two reusable Arabic QA corpora: HAQA for Hadith and QUQA for Quran questions, containing 1,598 and 3,382 question–answer pairs respectively. The method integrates existing datasets with expert-authored books, OCR, manual review, automated cleaning, metadata construction, and source/evidence fields distinguishing expert commentary from answer-bearing Quranic or Hadith passages. The evidence is the released corpus design, source-selection process, explicit credibility metadata, and stated coverage and size. Its limitations are that QA pairs are not annotated for argumentative roles, legal-school disagreement, or dialectical relations, and the mixture of source text and expert explanation can complicate faithfulness evaluation. It is relevant as a nearby benchmark and retrieval resource for Islamic textual systems, especially for testing citation grounding and answer extraction before attempting historically faithful modeling of kalām debates. [12]

## 13. “Computational Linguistic Analysis of the Biblical Text”
This book chapter surveys computational-linguistic approaches to biblical languages and textual analysis, including corpus methods, linguistic theories, databases, and computational representations of Biblical Hebrew and related scriptural languages. Its method is synthetic and methodological rather than a single new model: it compares how different linguistic frameworks shape computational analysis of the Biblical text. The evidence is the chapter’s discussion of existing computational resources, theories, and applications across biblical-language scholarship. Its limitation is that it provides enabling infrastructure and methodological orientation, not an annotated corpus or system for medieval Jewish scholastic argument, Christian *quaestiones*, or comparative theological debate. For this project, it supplies the comparative-tradition bridge: Hebrew and biblical NLP can support historical textual normalization and semantic analysis, but those capabilities must not be mistaken for computational modeling of Jewish scholastic reasoning. [13]

## 14. Tsarfaty et al., “What’s Wrong with Hebrew NLP? And How to Make it Right”
This paper presents ONLP/YAP, a joint morpho-syntactic parsing framework for Modern Hebrew designed to avoid error propagation in morphologically ambiguous Semitic text. The computational method jointly searches over morphological segmentation, disambiguation, part-of-speech and feature assignment, lemmas, and dependency trees using structured prediction and beam search rather than a brittle pipeline. The evidence is the system architecture, open implementation and output layers, and reported improvements over competing Hebrew models, with support for lattices and out-of-domain analysis. Its limitation is domain and task mismatch: it targets Modern Hebrew language processing and does not analyze medieval Jewish philosophy, rabbinic disputation, or arguments; performance also depends on lexicon coverage and annotated data. It is relevant as transferable infrastructure for Jewish scholastic corpora, where historically sensitive segmentation and syntactic analysis are prerequisites for reliable semantic and argument-level extraction. [14]

## 15. Mirarab and DarestaniFarahani, “Explainable Large Language Model for Islamic and Humanities Sciences Based on Knowledge Graphs”
This article proposes a conceptual framework integrating knowledge graphs with large language models for Islamic studies and the humanities, emphasizing explainability, conceptual inference, and representation of complex relations. The method is an analytical study based on a systematic literature review and purposeful selection of existing knowledge-graph and LLM resources; its stated output is a framework rather than a trained system. The evidence is the article’s explicit architecture, metadata, and claims that graph representations can organize concepts while LLMs extract and process natural-language information. Its central limitation is evidentiary: no publicly reported benchmark, corpus, ablation, expert agreement study, or historical kalām evaluation demonstrates that the proposed framework works in practice. It is nevertheless directly relevant as a design hypothesis for an ontology of doctrines, authorities, objections, distinctions, and inferential relations, provided the project treats it as a proposal rather than validated evidence. [15]

## 16. Mouhoub, “Islamic Large Language Models: From Knowledge Acquisition to Trustworthy and Hallucination-Resistant AI”
Mouhoub surveys Islamic LLMs and trustworthy Islamic AI across Arabic NLP, Islamic corpora, Quranic QA, knowledge benchmarks, retrieval-augmented generation, legal reasoning, inheritance reasoning, hallucination evaluation, and citation-aware generation. The method is a structured literature survey and research agenda organized around source hierarchy, exact citation verification, Classical-Arabic variation, madhhab-aware disagreement, retrieval, and expert evaluation. The evidence is the synthesis of existing models, resources, and benchmarks, together with the argument that fluency is insufficient without grounded sources and verifiable reasoning. Its limitation is that it is a survey rather than a new empirical benchmark or system, and its broad field map may include emerging resources whose comparative validity remains unsettled. For this project, it provides the clearest current specification of evaluation requirements: source-grounded extraction, explicit uncertainty and disagreement, citation accuracy, and tests against fabricated authorities and anachronistic reasoning. [16]

## 17. Lawrence and Reed, “Argument Mining: A Survey”
Lawrence and Reed define argument mining as the automatic identification and extraction of inferential structure expressed in natural language and review methods for detecting argumentative components and relations. The computational method surveyed includes discourse analysis, machine learning, structured prediction, component classification, relation identification, and increasingly deeper modeling of reasoning structure. The evidence is a broad synthesis of argument-mining tasks, datasets, techniques, and unresolved challenges across domains rather than a domain-specific Islamic or scholastic experiment. Its limitation is transfer: generic argument-mining categories and benchmarks do not automatically capture scholastic distinctions, authority relations, objections, replies, modality, genre conventions, or multilingual historical variation. It is the methodological anchor for this project because it defines the target capability, but it must be combined with expert-annotated corpora and historically grounded schemas for kalām, Jewish philosophy, and Christian *quaestiones*. [17]

## 18. Verification and project-level finding
The 17 retained items were checked against the supplied URLs and accessible publisher, repository, ACL, arXiv, Cambridge, Brill, OpenITI, and author-page records; the Hodges proof-search paper was additionally cross-checked against an accessible mirror because the retained author PDF did not download in the local manifest, and the chapter/PDF records with OCR restrictions were verified through bibliographic or publisher metadata. The set contains four symbolic/formalization studies, one computational Christian-scholastic protocol, Islamic-law text-mining and corpus infrastructure, Classical-Arabic and Hebrew NLP resources, Quran/Hadith QA and semantic datasets, one Islamic knowledge-graph/LLM proposal, a current Islamic-LLM survey, and a general argument-mining survey. Collectively, the evidence supports a sparse-and-fragmented literature finding: the strongest computational precedents formalize procedures or build enabling corpora, while no retained source provides a mature, openly benchmarked, multilingual AI system for historically faithful comparative modeling of kalām, Jewish scholastic argument, and Christian scholastic *quaestiones*.
