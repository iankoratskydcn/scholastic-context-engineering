# Subtopic 22: Scholastic Digital Humanities and AI — Verified Paper Summaries

## 1. Maliszewski, “It takes a village to write a book: Mapping anonymous contributions in Stephen Langton’s *Quaestiones Theologiae” (2025)

Maliszewski presents a proposed computational study of Stephen Langton’s *Quaestiones Theologiae*, a collection derived from reportationes of oral teaching, to investigate editorial layers and anonymous contributions. The workflow combines transformer-based handwritten-text recognition and transcription alignment with stylometric features such as frequent words, part-of-speech patterns, and pseudo-affixes, and it explicitly compares manually composed material with automatically extracted transcriptions. Its scholarly target is authorship attribution and reconstruction of the collection’s formation, not the recovery or validation of scholastic inferences; HTR and stylometry operate as evidence about textual production below the level of argument reasoning. The paper is therefore a direct precedent for computational analysis of a scholastic reportatio corpus, while remaining a study design whose results depend on transcription quality, corpus size, and the instability of stylometric signals in short questions. Verified record: [arXiv:2508.12830](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12830); publisher DOI listed in the source record: [10.1017/chr.2025.10004](https://doi.org/10.1017/chr.2025.10004).

## 2. Clérice, Vlachou-Efstathiou, and Chagué, “CREMMA Medii Aevi: Literary Manuscript Text Recognition in Latin” (2023)

CREMMA Medii Aevi contributes a segmentation and handwritten-text-recognition dataset for Medieval Latin literary manuscripts dating from the eleventh through the sixteenth centuries. The paper adds approximately 263,000 characters under guidelines designed to interoperate with earlier Latin and Medieval French HTR resources, with particular attention to abbreviations, and reports models that improve recognition on unknown Latin manuscripts. This is corpus digitization and AI-assisted transcription: the models make manuscript text more accessible, but they do not normalize the text, identify scholastic genre structure, reconstruct a quaestio, or reason over an argument. Its relevance to scholastic studies is infrastructural, since manuscript witnesses can become machine-readable inputs for later linguistic, transmission, or argument-level work; the dataset itself is broader than scholastic disputations. Verified records: [DOI 10.5334/johd.97](https://doi.org/10.5334/johd.97); [article PDF](https://malamatenia.github.io/pdfs/cremma-medii-aevi.pdf); [Zenodo dataset record](https://zenodo.org/records/7510411).

## 3. Koch et al., “A Tailored Handwritten-Text-Recognition System for Medieval Latin” (2023)

Koch and colleagues build an end-to-end HTR pipeline for handwritten lemma entries on the Bavarian Medieval Latin Dictionary’s record cards. The system uses image-segmentation models to locate relevant regions and transformer-based vision-encoder/GPT-2-decoder combinations to transcribe them, with extensive data augmentation; the reported best setup achieves a character error rate of 0.015 and is compared with Google Cloud Vision. The contribution is clearly AI transcription for a low-resource historical language rather than AI reasoning: it extracts lexical evidence from images but does not interpret scholastic propositions, represent disputational roles, or test inferences. Because the source material consists of dictionary cards rather than quaestiones or commentaries, its direct relevance is methodological—showing how domain-specific HTR can precede downstream Medieval Latin analysis—and it does not constitute a scholastic argument resource. Verified record: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.alp-1.12/); [paper PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2023.alp-1.12.pdf); [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09368).

## 4. McGillivray, Passarotti, and Ruffolo, “The Index Thomisticus Treebank Project: Annotation, Parsing and Valency Lexicon” (2009)

This paper introduces the Index Thomisticus Treebank as a dependency-annotated resource for Medieval Latin texts by Thomas Aquinas and describes its annotation, parsing experiments, and valency lexicon. The project turns a large Thomistic corpus into machine-readable morphosyntactic structures that support supervised parsing and lexical investigation. It is corpus infrastructure and NLP parsing, not AI reasoning: dependency trees identify syntactic relations and valency patterns, but they do not distinguish objections, authorities, *sed contra*, *respondeo*, replies, or the inferential warrants of a scholastic disputation. The treebank is nevertheless a foundational resource for computational work on Aquinas, including texts adjacent to *Sentences* commentary and disputed-question traditions, and it establishes that the relevant bottleneck is not merely access to Latin text but the addition of genre-aware and argument-level annotation. The source file’s author attribution should be corrected: the ACL record lists Barbara McGillivray, Marco Passarotti, and Paolo Ruffolo. Verified record: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2009.tal-2.5/).

## 5. Cecchini, Passarotti, Marongiu, and Zeman, “Challenges in Converting the Index Thomisticus Treebank into Universal Dependencies” (2018)

This paper revises the conversion of the Index Thomisticus Treebank into Universal Dependencies by harmonizing part-of-speech tags, lemmatization, and dependency relations with other Latin treebanks while correcting errors and inconsistencies in the earlier conversion. The authors evaluate the revised process against a manually checked gold standard and report improved conversion quality, especially for labeled attachment. The work concerns annotation normalization and interoperability, not reasoning: UD makes scholastic Latin accessible to broader NLP tools, but a standardized dependency representation is not a historically neutral account of medieval semantics and does not encode the architecture of a *quaestio* or assess argument validity. Its importance is methodological because it demonstrates that even apparently basic preprocessing decisions can materially affect downstream comparisons, retrieval, and parsing. Verified record: [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/W18-6004/); [paper PDF](https://aclanthology.org/W18-6004.pdf); [Zenodo repository record](https://zenodo.org/records/2552203).

## 6. Gamba, Passarotti, and Ruffolo, “More Data and New Tools: Advances in Parsing the Index Thomisticus Treebank” (2021)

Gamba, Passarotti, and Ruffolo evaluate how larger training data and newer parser combinations affect automatic dependency parsing of the Index Thomisticus Treebank. The experiments compare configurations including DeSR and neural parsers and examine ensemble behavior on Medieval Latin Aquinas data, showing that parser performance depends on language-specific modeling and that different systems reproduce characteristic errors. This is supervised AI/NLP at the syntactic level, not reasoning over scholastic arguments: improved attachment scores do not show that a model has reconstructed premises, objections, authorities, replies, or inferential force. The paper is useful as a benchmark precedent because it tests modern parsing assumptions against a historically and linguistically distinctive corpus, while leaving open the much harder problem of connecting parses to disputational structure and historically adequate argument interpretation. Verified record: [CEUR-WS paper PDF](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2989/long_paper20.pdf); [Zenodo DOI record](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5599095).

## 7. Mambrini, Passarotti, Moretti, and Pellegrini, “The Index Thomisticus Treebank as Linked Data in the LiLa Knowledge Base” (2022)

This LREC paper publishes annotation layers from the Index Thomisticus Treebank as Linguistic Linked Open Data within the LiLa Knowledge Base, linking Medieval Latin corpus tokens and dependency structures to interoperable lexical resources. The contribution is knowledge representation and corpus interoperability: persistent identifiers and explicit links make it possible to query text, morphology, syntax, and lexical information across resources. That infrastructure should not be mistaken for an argument graph or reasoning engine, because linked tokens and lexical relations do not by themselves encode premises, conclusions, objection/reply roles, or validity conditions. The work matters for scholastic AI because provenance-preserving links are a prerequisite for assembling contexts that retain the connection between a model’s input and its textual, lexical, and annotation sources. The source file’s author list should be corrected to the LREC record: Francesco Mambrini, Marco Passarotti, Giovanni Moretti, and Matteo Pellegrini. Verified record: [institutional publication record](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/210702); [LREC PDF](https://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2022/pdf/2022.lrec-1.428.pdf).

## 8. Nowak, Ziębura, Wróbel, and Smywiński-Pohl, “eFontes: Part of Speech Tagging and Lemmatization of Medieval Latin Texts: A Cross-Genre Survey” (2024)

The eFontes study develops transformer-based models for Medieval Latin lemmatization, part-of-speech tagging, and morphological-feature prediction using Universal Dependencies resources and a newly developed corpus of Polish Medieval Latin. It evaluates performance across genres and addresses orthographic variation and Latinized vernacular vocabulary, reporting strong but task-specific accuracy. This is AI/NLP annotation rather than reasoning: the models assign linguistic labels to tokens and do not infer the interpretation or argumentative role of a passage in a scholastic question. The paper is relevant because variation in spelling, vocabulary, and genre can propagate errors into search, quotation alignment, semantic modeling, and any later attempt at argument mining. Its results support treating linguistic annotation as an uncertainty-bearing layer rather than as a transparent substitute for historical reading. Verified record: [arXiv:2407.00418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00418).

## 9. *Corpus Thomisticum* official research infrastructure

The *Corpus Thomisticum* is a long-standing digital research infrastructure providing searchable editions of Aquinas’s works, bibliography, research tools, manuscript material, and database functions for words, phrases, quotations, similarities, correlations, and statistics. It is a corpus and retrieval platform rather than a scholarly paper or AI-reasoning system: its search and concordance capabilities enable discovery and comparison, but they do not perform argument-aware retrieval, reconstruct disputational roles, or validate the reasoning of a passage. For scholastic computational research, its significance is foundational because it supplies broad access to Latin texts and manuscript-oriented materials, while also making clear that searchable text is only the first layer of a reasoning-oriented pipeline. Any downstream model must still distinguish editions, witnesses, editorial choices, and passage-level evidence. Verified record: [official introduction](https://corpusthomisticum.org/wintroen.html).

## 10. Rockwell and Passarotti, “The Index Thomisticus as a Digital Humanities Big Data Project” (2019)

Rockwell and Passarotti analyze the Index Thomisticus as a major Digital Humanities infrastructure project, emphasizing its history, scale, data representation, concordance functions, and the project-management and media-archaeological conditions that made large-scale computational work on Aquinas possible. The paper is a history and analysis of digital infrastructure, not an AI model and not a formal account of scholastic reasoning. Its principal contribution to the present topic is to prevent anachronism: computational engagement with Aquinas predates current machine learning and demonstrates that digitization, indexing, and corpus-scale retrieval have their own long history. The article therefore weakens any novelty claim framed as simply “using computation on Aquinas” and redirects novelty toward provenance-preserving, genre-aware argument representation or evaluation. Verified record: [DOI 10.6092/issn.2532-8816/8575](https://doi.org/10.6092/issn.2532-8816/8575); [open article PDF](https://umanisticadigitale.unibo.it/article/download/8575/9248).

## 11. ERC-DEBATE, “Debate: Innovation as Performance in Late-Medieval Universities”

The ERC-DEBATE project studies late-medieval university *principia* as public academic performances and builds a corpus-oriented research program around authorship, transmission, neglected texts, and the intellectual dynamics of debate. The project’s CORDIS reporting describes *principia* as structured events combining oral performance, argument, authority, competition, and institutional setting, while the project corpus page frames the recovery and organization of texts across university contexts. This is corpus construction, digital editing, and manuscript/transmission research, not an evaluated AI-reasoning benchmark: identifying or attributing debate texts is different from extracting premises, modeling historically specific argumentative moves, or checking validity. Its importance is that it supplies a debate-centered corpus domain in which future argument-level annotation could be meaningful and provenance-sensitive. Verified records: [DEBATE project site](https://debate-erc.com/); [corpus page](https://debate-erc.com/corpus/); [CORDIS project record](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/771589).

## 12. University of Würzburg, *Arabic and Latin Corpus*

The University of Würzburg’s *Arabic and Latin Corpus* catalogs and digitizes Arabic–Latin translations from roughly the tenth through the fourteenth centuries, providing citable digital materials concerning texts, authors, translators, and witnesses. Its primary achievement is transmission infrastructure: it supports access, comparison, and historical study of cross-language movement rather than automatic reasoning about whether an Arabic argument and its Latin translation preserve the same premises, modality, quantification, or warrant. For scholastic computational work, the corpus is especially important because Arabic–Latin translation networks shaped the availability and vocabulary of medieval philosophy and science, yet bilingual alignment alone does not establish inferential equivalence. The resource therefore belongs in the prerequisite corpus layer, with argument-level translation evaluation remaining an open research problem. Verified records: [University of Würzburg project page](https://www.uni-wuerzburg.de/en/zpd/projects/); [Arabic and Latin Corpus site](https://www.arabic-latin-corpus.philosophie.uni-wuerzburg.de/).

## 13. UniQ: database of the Prague debate on universals

UniQ is a specialized database developed by the Institute of Philosophy of the Czech Academy of Sciences for the medieval debate on universals at the University of Prague between 1348 and 1500. It interlinks philosophical texts, authors, manuscripts, bibliographic sources, dating, authorship information, digital editions, and manuscript witnesses, and provides separate tools for manuscript and text records using linked-data principles. This is a debate-centered corpus and bibliographic graph, not AI reasoning: its relations describe scholarly and transmission entities rather than extracted premises, objections, replies, or formal validity judgments. UniQ is nevertheless a strong model for provenance-aware organization of a specific scholastic controversy and could serve as an upstream layer for argument annotation or model evaluation. Verified record: [Digital Humanities project page](https://digitalhumanities.cz/en/db/uniq-2/).

## 14. Dutilh Novaes, *Formalizing Medieval Logical Theories: Suppositio, Consequentiae and Obligationes* (2007)

Dutilh Novaes develops historically informed formal reconstructions of medieval theories of supposition, consequence, and obligationes, presenting supposition theory as algorithmic hermeneutics, analyzing consequence with tools related to model theory and two-dimensional semantics, and treating obligationes as logical games. Unlike the other retained items, this book directly addresses formal representations of historical reasoning, but it is a philosophical and interpretive formalization rather than a trained AI system or corpus-scale argument miner. Its central methodological value is its warning that formalization is not neutral transcription: the choice of representation must preserve the conceptual and logical practices of the historical tradition instead of silently replacing them with modern predicate logic. The book supplies the strongest retained precedent for actual reasoning-oriented modeling, while leaving open the alignment of formal structures with manuscript passages, textual variants, competing interpretations, and executable evaluation over large scholastic corpora. Verified record: [Springer book page](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-1-4020-5853-0).

## 15. Betti and van den Berg (eds.), *Data-Driven History of Ideas* and the e-Ideas program

The data-driven history-of-ideas program represented by Betti and van den Berg combines qualitative scholarship with quantitative and computational methods for tracing the origins, development, and spread of concepts, while emphasizing the need to represent concepts rather than merely count words and to build high-quality multilingual corpora. The related e-Ideas project combines ontology design, NLP-based machine learning, and distributional semantics under the principle that domain expertise must guide bottom-up corpus exploration. This is computational intellectual history and concept analysis, not direct argument reasoning over scholastic quaestiones: concept trajectories, topic distributions, and semantic proximity can reveal patterns but do not by themselves identify the argumentative function of a term inside an objection, authority citation, or *respondeo*. The methodological precedent is valuable for corpus-scale conceptual comparison, but transfer to medieval scholastic texts would require genre-aware segmentation, historical validation, and explicit separation of concept modeling from inference checking. Verified records: [Data-Driven History of Ideas collection](https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/collections/historyofideas); [e-Ideas project](https://conceptsinmotion.org/e-ideas/).

## Overall finding

Across the fifteen retained sources, the dominant contribution is infrastructure: digitized or searchable corpora, HTR, linguistic annotation, dependency parsing, linked data, authorship/transmission analysis, and debate or manuscript metadata. These systems are essential prerequisites for scholastic AI but should not be described as reasoning systems merely because they use transformers, machine learning, knowledge graphs, or statistical text analysis. Dutilh Novaes provides the clearest retained precedent for formalized historical reasoning, while Maliszewski provides the closest direct computational study of scholastic reportationes; neither supplies an evaluated end-to-end system that takes a medieval quaestio or disputation as input and produces a provenance-preserving, historically validated argument graph with checked inference. The defensible open space is therefore the missing middle between corpus infrastructure and formal reasoning: genre-aware argument-role annotation, explicit provenance and uncertainty, alignment between source passages and alternative formalizations, and evaluation that separates textual fidelity, historical adequacy, argument-form recognition, and logical validity.
