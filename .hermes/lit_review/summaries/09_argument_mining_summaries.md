# Subtopic 09 — Argument mining, argument components, and scheme classification

The ten retained papers below were checked against their primary PDFs or proceedings pages. Each paragraph records the paper’s method and principal finding, its main limitation, and its relation to the target novelty question: whether mined argument structure or schemes are used to select/compress proof-relevant context for LLM reasoning.

**Feng and Hirst (2011), “Classifying Arguments by Scheme.”** Feng and Hirst formulate argumentation-scheme classification as a supervised task over arguments whose premises and conclusions are already identified, using scheme-specific lexical and structural features to distinguish five common schemes; they report 63–91% one-against-others accuracy and 80–94% pairwise accuracy. The motivating downstream use is enthymeme reconstruction: classify the scheme, fit explicit propositions to its template, and infer unstated premises. The study is limited by its small Araucaria-derived corpus, restricted five-scheme inventory, upstream assumption that components are already detected, and low or incompletely reported annotation agreement. Relative to the target, it is a near-direct conceptual precedent for converting argument form into proof-relevant latent structure, but it neither retrieves or compresses context nor evaluates LLM reasoning. [Feng & Hirst 2011](https://aclanthology.org/P11-1099/)

**Lawrence and Reed (2016), “Argument Mining Using Argumentation Scheme Structures.”** Lawrence and Reed exploit the internal structure of argumentation schemes to classify proposition types and assign component roles, showing that scheme components can be recognized from segmented natural language without requiring a pre-existing argumentative graph; the reported component F-scores are approximately 0.78–0.91. This is more informative than a binary scheme label because it identifies how individual propositions instantiate a reasoning pattern and therefore supplies material for a deductive skeleton. Its limitations are the restricted scheme set and corpus, noisy or only partially analyzed input, and evaluation focused on component-role recognition rather than proof validity or downstream retrieval utility. It is one of the closest pre-LLM representation precedents for component detection plus scheme structure, but it does not use that representation to select or compress context for reasoning. [Lawrence & Reed 2016](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/ws/files/12346360/FAIA287_0379.pdf)

**Stab and Gurevych (2017), “Parsing Argumentation Structures in Persuasive Essays.”** Stab and Gurevych present a global argumentation-structure parser that jointly identifies token-level components and directed relations, using integer linear programming to impose coherence across component types and support/refutation links; the joint model improves over local or heuristic baselines on persuasive-essay data. The resulting graph is a strong substrate for tracing support and attack paths and for identifying candidate proof-relevant nodes. However, the work is domain-specific to persuasive essays, mainly represents claims/premises and support/attack rather than fine-grained inference schemes, and global coherence does not guarantee logical or proof validity; error analysis also shows substantial false positives caused by class imbalance and non-argumentative prose. Relative to the target, it is a core relation-mining precedent but not a scheme classifier, context compressor, or LLM reasoning system. [Stab & Gurevych 2017](https://aclanthology.org/J17-3005/)

**Lawrence and Reed (2020), “Argument Mining: A Survey.”** Lawrence and Reed survey the field’s foundations, datasets, and methods for argument/non-argument detection, component identification, relation mining, scheme analysis, and applications including retrieval and decision support. The survey establishes that claims, premises, directed relations, and scheme-level representations are mature and complementary strands rather than a single unified pipeline, and it catalogs recurring data and annotation challenges. As a survey, it introduces no new evaluated context-selection or reasoning method; moreover, its publication predates the current LLM and RAG wave, so it cannot by itself settle novelty against later prompt-compression systems. Its relation to the target is therefore architectural and historical: it confirms the availability of the representation layers that an integrated proof-oriented selector could combine, but contains no such closed loop. [Lawrence & Reed 2020](https://aclanthology.org/J19-4006/)

**Lauscher et al. (2021), “Argument Mining for Scholarly Document Processing: Taking Stock and Looking Ahead.”** Lauscher and colleagues survey argument mining for scientific and scholarly documents, covering argumentative discourse units, component types, and relations such as support, contradiction, same-claim, detail, and sequence, while emphasizing that scientific writing interprets evidence and persuades readers about conclusions. The paper’s principal contribution is to frame scholarly argument structure as a potentially useful representation for evidence interpretation, scientific retrieval, and knowledge construction, while identifying domain-specific data scarcity and annotation inconsistency. It is limited by sparse coverage of scientific discourse, heterogeneous label schemes across corpora, and the absence of an experiment showing that mined structure improves proof selection, context compression, or prompting. It is thus a near miss: it motivates structure-aware scientific context selection, but does not operationalize it and does not evaluate LLM reasoning. [Lauscher et al. 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.sdp-1.7.pdf)

**Fan et al. (2024), “FRVA: Fact-Retrieval and Verification Augmented Entailment Tree Generation for Explainable Question Answering.”** Fan et al. introduce FRVA, which first retrieves and filters irrelevant facts to shrink the proof-search space, then combines deductive and abductive bidirectional reasoning with cross-verification and contrastive learning to generate entailment trees. The method reports strong fact-selection and structural-correctness results on EntailmentBank and is the clearest inspected precedent for selecting only facts that participate in an explicit proof skeleton. Its limitations are the controlled EntailmentBank fact setting, concatenation of filtered facts into long model inputs, a termination condition that can miss proofs with multiple intermediate conclusions, and evaluation difficulties when several valid tree structures exist. Relative to the target, FRVA directly overlaps on proof-relevant context selection and verified deductive structure, but it does not mine natural-language argument components or classify Walton-style schemes; its inputs are already fact statements and entailment trees. [Fan et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.540/)

**Yue et al. (2024), “Retrieval Augmented Fact Verification by Synthesizing Contrastive Arguments (RAFTS).”** Yue et al. combine demonstration retrieval, document retrieval and reranking from verifiable sources, and in-context generation of supporting and refuting argument branches before an LLM makes a fact-verification prediction and explanation; the paper reports improvements over GPT-based baselines, including strong results with a smaller 7B model. RAFTS therefore demonstrates that retrieved evidence can be transformed into argument-shaped context rather than passed as undifferentiated passages. Its limitations are that retrieval robustness and reliability are not fully studied, domain mismatch between retrieval and fact-checking is not addressed, generated contrastive branches are not formally verified argument graphs, and no prompt-compression or proof-utility objective is measured. It is a direct overlap on argument-shaped RAG for LLM reasoning, but not on mined components, relations, schemes, or verified proof-skeleton selection. [Yue et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.556/)

**Ruiz-Dolz, Kikteva, and Lawrence (2025), “Mining Complex Patterns of Argumentative Reasoning in Natural Language Dialogue.”** Ruiz-Dolz et al. create QT-SCHEMES, a corpus of 891 extracted dialogue arguments, 441 of which receive one of 24 argumentation-scheme labels, and evaluate Transformer models and prompted/fine-tuned LLMs for scheme and scheme-family classification, including transfer from textbook-like scheme data to political debate. The work supplies a current fine-grained representation of premise-to-conclusion reasoning in natural dialogue and reports state-of-the-art scheme-mining results, making it the closest current precedent for the scheme layer of a proof-oriented system. Its main limitations are the small effective annotated set, severe class imbalance, expensive annotation, unstable scores across many classes, and weaker transfer when textbook-like patterns meet natural debate. It does not use predicted schemes to retrieve or compress context and does not test downstream proof construction or LLM reasoning over selected context, so it remains a representation precedent rather than an integrated selector. [Ruiz-Dolz et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.368/)

**Bezou-Vrakatseli, Cocarascu, and Modgil (2025), “Can Large Language Models Understand Argument Schemes?”** Bezou-Vrakatseli et al. systematically evaluate seven LLMs on Walton-taxonomy scheme classification under zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting, comparing prompts augmented with formal scheme definitions against prompts containing LLM-generated descriptions and testing manually annotated arguments, automatically generated arguments, and enthymemes. They find that larger models can classify schemes satisfactorily in suitable few-shot settings, while smaller models struggle and real, variable arguments are harder than structured generated examples. The study is limited to English, one taxonomy, a selected subset of schemes, and classification-only evaluation; it does not establish that scheme predictions improve reasoning, retrieval efficiency, or proof construction. Its novelty relation is direct for scheme classification in an LLM prompting regime, but the missing step is precisely the target integration: use scheme predictions to select or compress externally supplied proof context. [Bezou-Vrakatseli et al. 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.702/)

**Mushtaq and Cabessa (2025), “Argument Mining with Fine-Tuned Large Language Models.”** Mushtaq and Cabessa cast argument component classification, relation identification, relation classification, and joint end-to-end argument mining as text-generation tasks and fine-tune eight open-weight LLM configurations on the PE, AbstRCT, and CDCP benchmarks; they report state-of-the-art results across the principal AM subtasks and datasets, with structural/context tags helping some settings. The work demonstrates that a single fine-tuned LLM can recover component and support/attack structure without bespoke architectures or feature engineering, making it a practical baseline for constructing an argument graph. Its limitations include dependence on benchmark domains, model and hyperparameter choices, post-processing and formatting guardrails, incomplete statistical robustness analysis, and no evaluation of proof validity, scheme classification, retrieval, or context compression. Relative to the target, it directly covers LLM-based component/relation extraction but leaves the scheme layer and proof-relevant context-selection loop unimplemented. [Mushtaq & Cabessa 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.442/)
