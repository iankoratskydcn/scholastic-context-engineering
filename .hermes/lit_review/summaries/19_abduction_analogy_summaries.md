# Subtopic 19 — Abductive and Analogical Reasoning in LLMs

Verified against the publisher or author-hosted record on 2026-10-12. The source list contains 15 retained papers (not 17); the summaries below cover all 15 numbered entries in the agent brief. Two metadata corrections are applied: the first paper is by Paul and Frank, and the Occam paper's verified title is **Do Language Models Follow Occam’s Razor? An Evaluation of Parsimony in Inductive and Abductive Reasoning**.

## Abduction and hypothesis generation

1. **Paul & Frank (2021), “Generating Hypothetical Events for Abductive Inference” (STARSEM 2021).** The paper studies Abductive NLI as explanation selection and adds a specialized language model that generates plausible next events from hypothetical scenarios; a multitask model then compares event consequences associated with candidate hypotheses against the observed outcome. It reports improvement over vanilla pretrained language models fine-tuned on Abductive NLI, with manual analysis suggesting that modeling possible future events helps select explanations. The main limitation is task scope: this is constrained binary/choice-style abductive inference rather than open-ended hypothesis generation, and its notion of plausibility remains tied to the dataset’s event knowledge. For structured-context engineering, it establishes a useful observation → candidate explanation → consequence simulation pattern, showing why explicit temporal and causal bridges can improve abductive selection. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2021.starsem-1.6/

2. **Bai et al. (2024), “Advancing Abductive Reasoning in Knowledge Graphs through Complex Logical Hypothesis Generation” (ACL 2024).** The authors formulate complex logical hypothesis generation over knowledge graphs: a generated hypothesis must explain a set of observations through graph-supported relations. A supervised generator produces hypotheses structurally closer to references, but that objective does not reliably generalize to unseen observations; the proposed Reinforcement Learning from Knowledge Graph (RLF-KG) instead rewards agreement between observations and conclusions entailed by the graph, improving explanation quality and achieving state-of-the-art results on three knowledge graphs. The limitation is that structural closeness and graph entailment are proxies for explanatory adequacy, while performance depends on the completeness and correctness of the underlying KG. The paper directly supports relation-explicit context engineering: constraints and feedback tied to the knowledge structure are more useful than unconstrained generation. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.72/

3. **Zhou et al. (2024), “Hypothesis Generation with Large Language Models” (NLP4Science 2024).** This work generates initial scientific or data-driven hypotheses from a few labeled examples and iteratively updates them using predictive feedback and a multi-armed-bandit-inspired reward strategy, evaluating on synthetic and real datasets. The reported method improves over few-shot prompting and shows gains over supervised learning in some settings, indicating that LLM hypothesis generation benefits from an explicit propose–test–refine loop rather than one-shot completion. Its limitation is conceptual: predictive hypotheses are not necessarily explanatory, novel, or scientifically valid, so improvement in predictive reward does not establish genuine abduction. In relation to structured context, the paper motivates feeding models compact examples, evaluation feedback, and a controlled refinement objective, while warning that task reward can substitute for explanation unless the latter is measured separately. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2024.nlp4science-1.10/

4. **Sun & Saparov (2025), “Do Language Models Follow Occam’s Razor? An Evaluation of Parsimony in Inductive and Abductive Reasoning” (arXiv:2509.03345).** The paper introduces InAbHyD, a programmable synthetic benchmark with incomplete world models and observations, and evaluates hypotheses not only for correctness but also for parsimony under an Occam-style metric. It finds that language models can handle simple cases but struggle to produce high-quality, sufficiently simple hypotheses as worlds become more complex; in-context learning and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards do not eliminate the difficulty. The synthetic setup improves control and diagnostic resolution but limits ecological validity because it abstracts away noisy observations, open-world uncertainty, and domain-specific knowledge. The arXiv record checked on October 12, 2026 reports that the paper was accepted at the EMNLP 2026 main conference, although the accessible source remains the arXiv record. Its relation to context engineering is direct: abductive prompts should expose constraints, coverage, and simplicity trade-offs, not merely ask for a plausible explanation. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03345

5. **Zheng et al. (2025), “LogiDynamics: Unraveling the Dynamics of Inductive, Abductive and Deductive Logical Inferences in LLM Reasoning” (EMNLP 2025).** LogiDynamics evaluates inductive, abductive, and deductive inference in controlled textual, visual, and symbolic environments, varying difficulty and response format while examining hypothesis selection and iterative refinement. The study reports that explicit selection and refinement can substantially improve reasoning as task demands scale, and it places abduction within a broader family of logical inference dynamics rather than treating it as a single completion behavior. The controlled environments and analogy-oriented task framing limit direct transfer to unconstrained real-world explanations, and system-style labels should not be interpreted as evidence about human cognition. For structured contexts, the key implication is architectural: separate candidate generation, evidence-based selection, and refinement, then test each stage rather than treating fluent final text as proof of reasoning. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1045/

## Analogy identification, generation, and relational transfer

6. **Chen et al. (2022), “E-KAR: A Benchmark for Rationalizing Natural Language Analogical Reasoning” (Findings of ACL 2022).** E-KAR introduces a knowledge-intensive benchmark drawn from Chinese and English civil-service examinations, with 1,655 Chinese and 1,251 English problems, requiring both analogical question answering and free-text rationalization; candidate answers and explanations receive manual annotations. The authors find the benchmark challenging for contemporary models in both answer selection and explanation generation, making it difficult to be correct for the correct relational reason. Because the questions require substantial background knowledge, performance confounds analogy with retrieval and world knowledge, and generated rationales need not be faithful accounts of the model’s internal process. The benchmark is nevertheless highly relevant to context engineering because it requires relation-grounded explanations rather than surface-level answer matching. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-acl.311/

7. **Bhavya, Xiong & Zhai (2022), “Analogy Generation by Prompting Large Language Models: A Case Study of InstructGPT” (INLG 2022).** This study prompts InstructGPT to generate analogous concepts and explanations, comparing prompt wording, decoding temperature, and model scale. Precise imperative prompts and low temperature perform best; model size strongly affects quality, with the largest evaluated InstructGPT reaching human-level performance for concept generation while remaining weaker on explanation generation. The limitation is that judged fluency and appropriateness of generated analogies do not demonstrate relational transfer, causal analogy, or faithful reasoning, and the experiments reflect narrow tasks and an early instruction-tuned model family. Its contribution to structured-context design is controlled evidence that prompt form and decoding settings materially change analogy outputs, so these variables must be reported and held constant in comparisons. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2022.inlg-main.25/

8. **Hu et al. (2023), “In-Context Analogical Reasoning with Pre-Trained Language Models” (ACL 2023).** The authors convert visual Raven’s Progressive Matrices into language-based abstractions and use in-context prompts to select the correct completion, analyzing model complexity, demonstrations, and prior knowledge. The method shows how making perceptual relations explicit in text can enable pretrained language models to solve a visual analogy-style task, but textualization may measure language-mediated reconstruction rather than native visual analogy, and results depend heavily on the quality of the abstraction and available prior knowledge. The paper therefore provides a direct structured-context intervention: relational descriptions can help, but the representation itself becomes part of the task and may carry much of the difficulty or answer signal. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.109/

9. **Wijesiriwardene et al. (2023), “ANALOGICAL: A Novel Benchmark for Long Text Analogy Evaluation in Large Language Models” (Findings of ACL 2023).** ANALOGICAL benchmarks whether models can identify analogous situations across extended narratives, targeting analogy beyond short word or sentence pairs. Its method stresses long-context reading and relational retrieval, making it useful for separating short-range matching from story-scale analogy identification. The central limitation is construct overlap: success requires memory, retrieval, reading comprehension, and narrative abstraction in addition to analogy, so poor performance cannot be attributed to relational mapping alone. For context engineering, the benchmark underscores that adding more context does not guarantee better transfer; systems need mechanisms that retrieve and align the relevant relational structure within long inputs. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.218/

10. **Yuan et al. (2023), “StoryAnalogy: Deriving Story-level Analogies from Large Language Models to Unlock Analogical Understanding” (EMNLP 2023).** StoryAnalogy constructs a 24,000 story-pair corpus with human annotations based on an extended Structure-Mapping Theory and evaluates both story-level analogy identification and generation. The reported ChatGPT result is about 30% on a multiple-choice identification task, compared with more than 85% for humans, revealing a large gap in mapping extended narrative relations. The limitation is that story analogies combine long-context retrieval, commonsense interpretation, linguistic variation, and annotation complexity, so the gap is not a pure measure of analogy failure. The paper contributes a relationally annotated resource for testing whether models align event roles and structures rather than merely matching topical vocabulary, making it especially relevant to structured narrative context. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.706/

11. **Petersen & van der Plas (2023), “Can Language Models Learn Analogical Reasoning? Investigating Training Objectives and Comparisons to Human Performance” (EMNLP 2023).** The study tests several training objectives on basic analogies designed to resemble human analogical-reasoning evaluations rather than standard word-embedding analogy tasks, and compares trained models with a human-baseline dataset. Models learn the evaluated behavior from relatively small amounts of data and approach human performance after training. The limitation is that small, basic, supervised tasks show learnability under task-specific training but do not establish zero-shot, cross-domain, or compositional relational transfer. Its relevance to context engineering is diagnostic: explicit training or examples can teach an analogy task, so evaluations must distinguish memorized task behavior from general reasoning and should include held-out relations and domains. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.1022/

12. **Ye et al. (2024), “AnaloBench: Benchmarking the Identification of Abstract and Long-context Analogies” (EMNLP 2024).** AnaloBench collects 340 human-written analogies and evaluates 12 open-source and three proprietary models on two human-relevant abilities: recalling related experiences from a large pool and applying analogy to complex, lengthy scenarios. Scaling produces some improvements, but yields minimal gains for lengthy scenarios and for retrieving relevant analogues from large information pools. The benchmark is modest in size and its human-written scenarios impose a particular domain and difficulty distribution, which limits broad generalization claims. It directly informs retrieval-aware context engineering by separating analogue recall from mapping and transfer, showing that larger models alone do not solve the long-context bottleneck. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.725/

13. **Qin et al. (2025), “Relevant or Random: Can LLMs Truly Perform Analogical Reasoning?” (Findings of ACL 2025).** This paper compares self-generated relevant examples with irrelevant or random examples before solving reasoning tasks, testing whether improvements arise from genuinely useful analogical context rather than from adding any extra text. The experimental design treats generated exemplars as candidate analogical supports and finds that context relevance matters, supporting a relevance-sensitive account of analogical prompting. A limitation is that relevant examples can still provide semantic priming, lexical cues, or task hints, and operational relevance in the benchmark is not a complete theory of relational analogy. The practical relation is strong: context selection should be evaluated against random and irrelevant controls, and gains should not be attributed to analogy unless mapping and transfer are separately demonstrated. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.1230/

14. **Wijesiriwardene et al. (2025), “KnowledgePrompts: Exploring the Abilities of Large Language Models to Solve Proportional Analogies via Knowledge-Enhanced Prompting” (COLING 2025).** KnowledgePrompts introduces a 15,000-item proportional-analogy multiple-choice dataset and compares exemplar prompts with structured and targeted knowledge prompts. The reported best model reaches 55%, and targeted knowledge performs better than the other knowledge formats, indicating that the content and granularity of context matter more than simply adding demonstrations. The limitation is that multiple-choice proportional analogies may not measure open-ended relational transfer, while targeted knowledge can encode much of the relation needed to select the answer. For context engineering, the paper provides direct evidence for relation-explicit, targeted context over undifferentiated example dumps, while also motivating controls for answer leakage and knowledge sufficiency. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.268/

15. **Das & Balke (2025), “From Prototypical to Relational: How LLMs Navigate Complex Analogies” (INLG 2025).** The authors build a four-way complex-analogy benchmark in which all answer choices are semantically plausible, using relations from Wikidata and AnalogyKB and evaluating relational overlap alongside embedding similarity and prototypicality. Models including GPT-4 perform better on embedding- and prototype-based measures than on fine-grained relational mappings, indicating that plausible surface similarity can mask weak structure alignment. The benchmark’s relation mining and GPT-4-assisted salience ranking introduce pipeline and model dependence, while making every option plausible reduces the evaluation to a less sharply defined ground truth than ordinary multiple choice. Its contribution is a clean diagnostic separation between prototypical similarity and relational correspondence, which is essential when testing whether structured context actually supports analogy rather than merely improving topical matching. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2025.inlg-main.28/

## Cross-paper implications

Across these 15 studies, relation-explicit context consistently appears more useful than undifferentiated examples, but context can help through retrieval, priming, representation, or answer cues without proving relational reasoning. Abductive systems should therefore score coverage/consistency, parsimony, and unseen-case generalization; analogy systems should separately measure analogue retrieval, structure mapping, and transfer. Fluent explanations are insufficient evidence of faithful reasoning, so robust evaluations should include irrelevant-context controls, distractors, alternative plausible hypotheses, long-context tests, and cross-domain or unseen-relation transfer.
