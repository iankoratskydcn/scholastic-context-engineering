# Subtopic 12 — Deductive and syllogistic benchmark summaries

## 1. Johnson-Laird & Bara (1984), “Syllogistic Inference”
Johnson-Laird and Bara’s benchmark is a foundational human experimental regime for categorical syllogisms and three-term series problems, varying premise figure—the arrangement of terms—and the number of mental models required by an inference. Their experiments found systematic effects of figure and model count on performance and supported a mental-model account in which reasoners construct a model, derive a candidate conclusion, and search for counterexamples. Its limitation is that it is a small, classical human-cognition study rather than an LLM benchmark, and its theory and stimuli predate contemporary natural-language evaluation protocols. It remains directly relevant because any LLM syllogism benchmark that manipulates term order, figure, validity, or model complexity is extending a test structure already established in this work. Source: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0010027784900350

## 2. Oakhill, Johnson-Laird & Garnham (1989), “Believability and Syllogistic Reasoning”
This paper tests belief bias in syllogistic reasoning by examining whether believability affects premise interpretation, the examination of alternative representations, or the final filtering of candidate conclusions. Across three experiments involving both conclusion generation and conclusion evaluation, participants were influenced by beliefs at the alternative-model and conclusion-filtering stages, not only during initial premise interpretation. The limitation is that the evidence concerns human subjects and controlled syllogistic materials, so it does not directly quantify LLM reasoning or distinguish all modern failure modes such as parsing and format errors. Its relevance is methodological: benchmark items should cross logical validity with believable, unbelievable, and neutral content, because an accuracy score alone can hide whether a system is following logic or accepting plausible conclusions. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2721132/

## 3. Clark, Tafjord & Richardson (2020), “Transformers as Soft Reasoners over Language” (RuleTaker)
RuleTaker evaluates whether transformers can infer the truth of assertions from natural-language facts and rules generated from a controlled theory, with theorem-prover-derived labels and explicit reasoning-depth splits. The reported models reached about 99% accuracy on standard settings, above 95% on substantially deeper chains than seen during training, and transferred to hand-authored and paraphrased rulebases. Its limitations are the synthetic grammar, restricted vocabulary, and narrow rule language; it tests rule chaining rather than categorical syllogistic moods, open-ended argument interpretation, or rich proof quality. The benchmark is nevertheless a canonical baseline for deductive LLM evaluation because it makes depth, closed-world assumptions, and generalization measurable and establishes the soft-theorem-prover framing. Source: https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/2020/537

## 4. Tafjord, Dalvi Mishra & Clark (2021), “ProofWriter: Generating Implications, Proofs, and Abductive Statements over Natural Language”
ProofWriter extends RuleTaker-style natural-language theories from classifying preselected claims to generating logical implications, natural-language proofs, and constrained abductive explanations. It evaluates depth-controlled datasets, including unseen-depth and out-of-domain settings, and reports proof accuracy exceeding earlier reconstruction methods by 9 percentage points on RuleTaker while also providing strong baselines for implication enumeration and abduction. The limitation is that the theories remain largely templated and Datalog-like, so exact proof correctness does not test nuanced interpretation of real arguments, quantifier ambiguity, or human content effects. The work is highly relevant to benchmark design because it demonstrates that answer labels, proof certificates, consequence enumeration, and abduction should be treated as distinct evaluation targets rather than collapsed into one accuracy number. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-acl.317.pdf

## 5. Tian et al. (2021), “Diagnosing the First-Order Logical Reasoning Ability Through LogicNLI”
LogicNLI is an NLI-style first-order-logic benchmark built from facts, rules, and hypotheses, covering combinations of core logical operators while minimizing commonsense interference; it adds a “Paradox” relation for cases where both a proposition and its negation can be derived through different paths. BERT, RoBERTa, and XLNet are evaluated across accuracy, robustness, generalization, and interpretability/traceability, and the reported models perform above random but below human performance, exposing weaknesses in FOL reasoning. Its limitation is the controlled logical language and pre-generative-LLM model regime, with no direct coverage of classical syllogistic figure or belief-bias effects. Its relevance is the diagnostic separation of entailment, contradiction, neutral, and inconsistency-like cases, which supports richer labels and robustness tests in a new deductive benchmark. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.303/

## 6. Han et al. (2022/2024), “FOLIO: Natural Language Reasoning with First-Order Logic”
FOLIO evaluates deductive validity over human-authored natural-language premise sets and conclusions paired with first-order-logic annotations; its 1,430 unique conclusions are drawn from 487 premise sets, and an inference engine automatically verifies the formal annotations. The dataset supports both natural-language reasoning and NL-to-FOL translation, and the paper reports systematic experiments across multiple language models, including evidence that a difficult subset challenges GPT-4. Its limitations are that conclusion-level validity and translation accuracy still do not fully measure proof structure, minimal premise use, or alternative valid derivations, while formal annotation imposes an intended interpretation on potentially ambiguous language. FOLIO is relevant as a strong naturalness and logical-diversity baseline and as precedent for evaluating parsing/formalization separately from downstream deduction. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00840

## 7. Saparov & He (2022), “Language Models Are Greedy Reasoners: A Systematic Formal Analysis of Chain-of-Thought” (PrOntoQA)
PrOntoQA generates question-answering problems from synthetic first-order world models so that a model’s chain of thought can be parsed into symbolic proof steps and analyzed by proof depth. Experiments with GPT-3 and InstructGPT found that models can often execute individual deduction steps correctly, including in fictional settings, but struggle with proof planning when multiple valid next steps must be explored. Its limitation is the restricted ontology and largely repeated modus-ponens-style structure, which makes it a controlled proof-depth test rather than a broad benchmark of categorical syllogisms, natural argumentation, or belief effects. It is relevant because it provides a clean precedent for separating final-answer accuracy from proof validity, search/planning behavior, and chain-of-thought faithfulness. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01240

## 8. Srivastava et al. (2022), “Beyond the Imitation Game: Quantifying and Extrapolating the Capabilities of Language Models” (BIG-bench)
BIG-bench is a collaborative suite of 204 tasks evaluated across model families and scales, with human-rater baselines and task families covering logical deduction, categorical/formal syllogisms, and logical fallacies. Its results show broad scale-related improvement but generally poor absolute performance and calibration relative to human raters, while some multistep or brittle tasks display non-smooth capability changes. The limitation is that task-level binary or multiple-choice scoring can conflate reasoning with parsing and format compliance, and some tasks do not expose unknown, contradiction, proof, or error-type distinctions. BIG-bench is relevant as an ecosystem-level precedent for standardized zero-/few-shot evaluation and for placing syllogistic validity, formal fallacies, and general logical deduction in a common test suite. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615

## 9. Sanyal, Singh & Ren (2022), “FaiRR: Faithful and Robust Deductive Reasoning over Natural Language”
FaiRR evaluates deductive reasoning over natural-language rulebases by decomposing the task into independently modeled rule selection, fact selection, and knowledge composition components, thereby creating an explicit causal route from selected premises to generated inferences. On existing reasoning datasets, the authors report robustness to novel language perturbations, faster inference than prior approaches, and more interpretable errors than black-box generative proof systems. Its limitation is that the underlying rule-based regime remains controlled and does not directly address categorical quantifier moods, belief bias, premise-order effects, or open-ended argument structure. The work is relevant to benchmark construction because it motivates scoring evidence selection and derivation faithfulness separately from the final label and provides a model-side template for diagnosing whether a failure arose from premise selection or composition. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2022.acl-long.77/

## 10. Pan et al. (2023), “Logic-LM: Empowering Large Language Models with Symbolic Solvers for Faithful Logical Reasoning”
Logic-LM evaluates a pipeline in which an LLM translates a natural-language problem into a symbolic representation, a deterministic solver performs inference, and solver feedback drives self-refinement. It is tested on ProofWriter, PrOntoQA, FOLIO, LogicalDeduction, and AR-LSAT, achieving an average improvement of 39.2% over standard prompting and 18.4% over chain-of-thought prompting. Its limitations are dependence on the expressiveness of the selected symbolic solver and on in-context translation/self-refinement, which can fail for intricate or poorly expressible logical grammars; solver success also does not guarantee that the initial formalization captured the intended meaning. Its relevance is as a direct precedent for reporting parser/formalization errors separately from solver errors and for evaluating deductive benchmarks with hybrid symbolic verification. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.248/

## 11. Wu et al. (2023), “Hence, Socrates is mortal: A Benchmark for Natural Language Syllogistic Reasoning” (SylloBase)
SylloBase is a direct natural-language syllogism benchmark designed to cover a complete taxonomy of syllogistic reasoning patterns and both generation and understanding tasks. It contains 50,000 template-based samples mined from Wikidata and ConceptNet, GPT-3 paraphrases, and 1,000 manually rewritten test examples; T5 obtains a best generation ROUGE-L of 38.72 and RoBERTa a best multiple-choice accuracy of 72.77%. Its limitations are artifact risk from template construction and GPT-3 paraphrasing, plus an emphasis on answer prediction rather than proof generation, minimal-premise use, or invariance under irrelevant premise/order changes. It is highly relevant as an existing baseline against which any new categorical-syllogism benchmark must demonstrate added value beyond taxonomy coverage and surface naturalness. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-acl.148/

## 12. Ando et al. (2023), “Evaluating Large Language Models with NeuBAROCO: Syllogistic Reasoning Ability and Human-like Biases”
NeuBAROCO repurposes a dataset originally created for psychological experiments into English- and Japanese-language LLM evaluations of categorical syllogisms, including belief bias, conversion errors, and atmosphere effects. The experiments find that current LLMs struggle more on problems involving these human-associated bias conditions, linking model behavior to established cognitive-science phenomena rather than only measuring aggregate validity. Its limitations are the need to disentangle dataset provenance, translation/language effects, and genuine bias resemblance; reproducing a human error pattern is not evidence of logically valid reasoning. The paper is relevant because it establishes multilingual, psychologically grounded bias testing as a benchmark dimension and supports evaluating content effects and indeterminate relations alongside correctness. Source: https://aclanthology.org/2023.naloma-1.1.pdf

## 13. Ozeki et al. (2024), “Exploring Reasoning Biases in Large Language Models Through Syllogism: Insights from the NeuBAROCO Dataset”
This follow-up NeuBAROCO study evaluates English and Japanese syllogistic problems using entailment, contradiction, and neutral cases, and adds a Translate-and-Explain chain-of-thought procedure that asks models to formalize the syllogism before explaining their reasoning. The results report human-like reasoning biases, additional model-specific error tendencies, and especially poor performance when the relation is neither entailment nor contradiction; analysis suggests that reasoning-process failures are more important than basic syllogism interpretation errors. Its limitation is that it is closely related to the 2023 NeuBAROCO report rather than an independent benchmark family, and the translation-plus-explanation protocol may itself introduce prompt and language artifacts. It is relevant as evidence that neutral/indeterminate cases and separate parsing-versus-reasoning diagnostics are essential for a modern syllogistic benchmark. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04403

## 14. Eisape et al. (2023/2024), “A Systematic Comparison of Syllogistic Reasoning in Humans and Language Models”
This study compares human behavior with PaLM 2 and related language-model variants on two-premise categorical syllogisms, probing model scale, premise/term ordering, and classic syllogistic fallacies. Larger PaLM 2 models are reported to be more logical than smaller models and, on the studied items, more logical than humans, but even the largest models remain sensitive to irrelevant variable ordering and make confident invalid inferences that resemble human biases. Its limitations are the time-specific model family and prompt protocol, and human-like behavior is not necessarily the target for an LLM benchmark because humans can be systematically wrong. Its relevance is particularly strong for paired invariance and human/model evaluation: it shows that aggregate accuracy must be supplemented with order perturbations, fallacy profiles, calibration, and confidence/error analysis. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00445

## 15. Abe et al. (2024/2025), “Abductive Reasoning with Syllogistic Forms in Large Language Models”
This paper converts a syllogistic dataset into an abductive task in which models choose which candidate premise best explains an observed conclusion, while also comparing abductive and deductive judgments. The released data encode English premises and hypotheses, gold abductive and deductive labels, syllogistic figure/form and term-order metadata, contradiction labels, and belief-consistency categories; the work frames abduction as a complementary form of reasoning rather than a replacement for deduction. Its limitation is conceptual as well as empirical: explanatory adequacy is not deductive validity, and the publication metadata differ across the Springer/HAR record and the later arXiv version, so bibliography details require care. It is relevant as an adjacent contrast benchmark that can test whether an LLM distinguishes necessary consequence from plausible explanation instead of silently pooling both under “reasoning accuracy.” Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06428
