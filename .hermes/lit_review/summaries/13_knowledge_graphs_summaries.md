# Subtopic 13 — Knowledge graphs and structured knowledge as LLM scaffolding

## Verified paper summaries

### 1. ERNIE: Enhanced Representation through Knowledge Integration — Sun et al. (2019)
ERNIE adapts BERT-style masked-language pretraining so that the masking units reflect linguistic and knowledge-bearing structure rather than isolated tokens. Its entity-level masking hides multi-token entities as a unit, while phrase-level masking hides contiguous conceptual phrases, encouraging the model to recover semantically meaningful knowledge from context. The paper reports improvements over baseline models on five Chinese NLP tasks, including natural-language inference, semantic similarity, named-entity recognition, sentiment analysis, and question answering, as well as stronger cloze-style knowledge inference. As a scaffold, ERNIE is internal rather than inspectable at inference time: structured knowledge is absorbed into model representations during pretraining, which improves downstream competence but does not expose a query-specific evidence graph or guarantee that a generated claim can be traced to a particular fact. [10]

### 2. K-BERT: Enabling Language Representation with Knowledge Graph — Liu et al. (AAAI 2020)
K-BERT injects knowledge-graph triples directly into an input sentence, expanding the original text into a knowledge-rich tree that a BERT encoder can process without full domain-specific pretraining. The method addresses two practical problems created by injection: heterogeneous representations between textual tokens and graph entities, and knowledge noise caused by irrelevant expansions. It uses soft positions to preserve the original sentence structure and a visible matrix to restrict which tokens may attend to injected knowledge, thereby limiting interference with the sentence’s meaning. The paper reports gains across twelve NLP tasks and emphasizes that the injected knowledge remains manually editable. K-BERT therefore establishes a clear side-context scaffold, but its sentence expansion and encoder-oriented design leave open the harder problem of selecting and verifying evidence for unconstrained, open-ended generation. [9]

### 3. KEPLER: A Unified Model for Knowledge Embedding and Pre-trained Language Representation — Wang et al. (2020)
KEPLER unifies language-model pretraining with knowledge-graph embedding by encoding textual descriptions of entities with a pretrained language model and jointly optimizing language-modeling and relational knowledge-embedding objectives. This design lets the model use textual information to improve entity representations while also imposing graph-based constraints that capture relations among entities. The authors introduce Wikidata5M, a large-scale knowledge graph aligned with entity descriptions, and report strong results on language tasks and knowledge-graph link prediction, including inductive settings involving entities not seen during training. KEPLER is important as a shared-representation precursor to graph-conditioned language systems: it places language and structured knowledge in a common latent space rather than constructing a runtime prompt. Its limitation for context engineering is that joint embeddings do not themselves expose which graph facts support a particular answer or enforce faithful multi-hop generation. [11]

### 4. QA-GNN: Reasoning with Language Models and Knowledge Graphs for Question Answering — Yasunaga et al. (NAACL 2021)
QA-GNN addresses knowledge-intensive multiple-choice question answering by combining language-model relevance estimation with graph reasoning over a retrieved knowledge-graph subgraph. Given a question and answer choice, it first identifies topic entities and extracts a bounded subgraph, then scores graph nodes according to their relevance to the QA context. The model represents the textual QA context as a node in a joint graph connected to the retrieved KG entities, and an attention-based graph network updates the language and graph representations together. Evaluations on CommonsenseQA and OpenBookQA show improvements over language-model-only and prior LM-plus-KG systems, including better handling of negation and entity substitution; the paper also presents reasoning subgraphs as a form of explanation. QA-GNN is a strong query-specific scaffold, although its evidence selection and reasoning are specialized to bounded QA rather than free-form generation. [12]

### 5. GreaseLM: Graph REASoning Enhanced Language Models for Question Answering — Zhang et al. (ICLR 2022)
GreaseLM builds a deeper fusion between language models and graph neural networks for questions that require both textual interpretation and structured world knowledge. Instead of adding a graph representation only before or after language encoding, it stacks cross-modal interaction layers in which information propagates in both directions: graph representations ground the language context, while linguistic features such as negation and hedging modify the graph-side reasoning. The authors evaluate the approach on CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, and the medical MedQA-USMLE benchmark, reporting more reliable reasoning over situational constraints and structured knowledge and performance that can exceed models substantially larger than GreaseLM. The method demonstrates that a KG can function as an active reasoning partner rather than a static prompt supplement. However, it depends on curated graph construction and task-specific architecture, and its learned fusion does not by itself provide claim-level provenance for generated answers. [13]

### 6. Knowledge Graph Prompting for Multi-Document Question Answering — Wang et al. (AAAI 2024)
Knowledge Graph Prompting (KGP) targets multi-document question answering, where the relevant evidence is distributed across passages and document structures rather than contained in one independently retrievable chunk. The method constructs a graph whose nodes represent passages or structural units such as pages and tables, with edges encoding semantic or lexical similarity and document relations. An LLM-based graph-traversal agent then navigates this global graph to collect supporting passages, using the graph as a global regulator of transitions and the agent as a local navigator that progressively approaches the question. The paper reports improved retrieval and QA performance and argues that graph organization can reduce retrieval latency while preserving cross-document associations. KGP is especially close to context engineering because it separates graph construction, traversal, and prompt formation, but its final evidence selection still depends on learned extraction and LLM navigation rather than formal entailment checks. [3]

### 7. MindMap: Knowledge Graph Prompting Sparks Graph of Thoughts in Large Language Models — Wen, Wang, and Sun (ACL 2024)
MindMap presents a plug-and-play prompting pipeline that uses knowledge graphs not only as external evidence but also as a scaffold for organizing the model’s intermediate reasoning. The system extracts keywords from the question, identifies corresponding KG entities, retrieves path and neighborhood evidence, merges those subgraphs into reasoning structures, and prompts an LLM to combine external KG facts with its implicit knowledge. It additionally elicits a “mind map” of the inference process, intended to make the reasoning pathway more transparent and graph-structured. Experiments across question-answering tasks, with particular attention to medical settings, report improvements over prompting baselines and introduce a hallucination-oriented evaluation benchmark. MindMap’s distinctive contribution is topological organization of evidence and thoughts, but its reasoning transformations remain model-mediated: graph completeness, consistency, and correspondence between the displayed mind map and the actual causal basis of the answer are not guaranteed. [2]

### 8. KnowGPT: Knowledge Graph based Prompting for Large Language Models — Feng et al. (NeurIPS 2024)
KnowGPT develops a knowledge-graph prompting framework for augmenting closed or black-box language models with domain knowledge using hard prompts. It is motivated by the large search spaces, API costs, and manual prompt-engineering burden that arise when an LLM must decide how to use KG information. The framework includes a knowledge-extraction module that selects informative facts from a graph and a context-aware prompt-construction module that converts those facts into prompts suitable for the target LLM. The authors evaluate KnowGPT on three benchmarks and report substantial gains, including 92.6% accuracy on OpenBookQA. The paper’s main relevance to scaffolding is its explicit separation of graph knowledge selection from prompt realization, allowing structured domain facts to reach an otherwise unchanged LLM. The remaining risks are entity-linking and path-selection errors, sensitivity to KG quality, and the absence of a guarantee that the model’s response is entailed by the prompted facts. [1]

### 9. KAPALM: Knowledge grAPh enhAnced Language Models for Fake News Detection — Ma et al. (Findings EMNLP 2023)
KAPALM applies graph-enhanced language modeling to fake-news detection, showing that structured entity relations can support credibility-sensitive classification beyond simple entity matching. The system links entities in a news item to a knowledge graph, extracts a relevant subgraph, and computes a coarse-grained representation with a graph neural network. It then prunes the subgraph and processes the resulting fine-grained knowledge with attentive graph and graph-pooling layers before combining both graph representations with the textual representation of the news. On two benchmark datasets, the authors report results superior to state-of-the-art baselines and competitive performance in a few-shot setting. KAPALM broadens the evidence for graph scaffolds beyond QA and generation: graph structure can supply relational signals for a decision task. Its direct transfer to general-purpose LLM context construction is limited, however, because the architecture, supervision, and evaluation are tightly tied to fake-news classification and do not test generation faithfulness. [8]

### 10. Knowledge Graph-Enhanced Large Language Models via Path Selection — Liu et al. (Findings ACL 2024)
This paper proposes KELP, a framework for selecting knowledge-graph paths before incorporating them into LLM processing. It responds to two weaknesses in earlier KG-enhanced methods: reliance on the LLM to make coarse binary decisions about whether a path should be used, and a tendency to select only paths with direct lexical or semantic overlap with the input. KELP assigns finer-grained scores to candidate paths through latent semantic matching and uses trained encodings to preserve useful paths whose relations to the input are indirect. Experiments on real-world datasets support the effectiveness of this more principled selection process. For structured context engineering, KELP isolates a key control point between graph retrieval and model consumption: the scaffold should be compact, relevance-ranked, and capable of preserving inferentially useful indirect relations. The remaining concern is that learned path scoring can encode selection bias and may omit conflicting or complementary evidence outside the chosen paths. [7]

### 11. GRAG: Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation — Hu et al. (Findings NAACL 2025)
GRAG extends retrieval-augmented generation from isolated documents to networked textual graphs such as citation networks, social graphs, and knowledge graphs. It treats retrieval as a textual-subgraph problem and proposes a divide-and-conquer strategy intended to identify a useful subgraph efficiently, with linear-time retrieval in the paper’s formulation. For generation, GRAG exposes the retrieved graph through complementary text and graph views so that the LLM can use both node content and topology. Experiments on graph-reasoning benchmarks show especially strong gains over conventional RAG in multi-hop settings where relationships among documents are essential. GRAG’s central contribution is to make topology part of the retrieved context rather than treating edges as incidental metadata. This broadens KG scaffolding to arbitrary networked corpora, but neighborhood expansion can still introduce noise and context cost, and the reported benefits depend on graphs whose topology is meaningfully aligned with answer relevance. [4]

### 12. Knowledge Graph-Guided Retrieval Augmented Generation — Zhu et al. (NAACL 2025)
KG²RAG improves retrieval-augmented generation by using a knowledge graph to connect otherwise isolated text chunks. It begins with semantic retrieval to obtain seed chunks, then performs KG-guided chunk expansion to find factually related evidence and KG-based organization to arrange the selected material into coherent paragraphs for the generator. The framework is designed to improve both retrieval quality and response quality by preserving fact-level relationships, increasing evidence diversity, and supplying the LLM with a more structured context than a ranked list of semantically similar chunks. Experiments on HotpotQA and variants report advantages over existing RAG methods on both retrieval and answer measures. KG²RAG is a direct model of graph-mediated context construction, but it remains primarily a retrieval optimization layer: it assumes reliable graph construction and entity/chunk linking, can propagate graph errors, and does not eliminate unsupported claims produced after the organized context reaches the LLM. [5]

### 13. Graph-constrained Reasoning: Faithful Reasoning on Knowledge Graphs with Large Language Models — Luo et al. (ICML 2025)
Graph-constrained reasoning (GCR) moves from presenting graph evidence to constraining the language model’s reasoning process so that generated paths remain grounded in a knowledge graph. Its KG-Trie is a trie-based index of valid KG reasoning paths that is integrated into decoding, restricting the model toward graph-valid continuations rather than allowing unconstrained free-form reasoning. The framework combines a lightweight KG-specialized LLM for graph-constrained reasoning with a stronger general LLM for inductive reasoning across multiple candidate paths. On several KGQA benchmarks, the paper reports state-of-the-art results, zero reasoning hallucination under its graph-grounded formulation, and zero-shot generalization to unseen KGs without additional training. GCR represents a stronger scaffold than retrieval or prompting because graph structure becomes a decoding constraint. The tradeoff is brittleness: incomplete, ambiguous, or poorly matched graphs can reduce recall and flexibility, so faithfulness is bounded by graph coverage and entity/linking quality. [6]
