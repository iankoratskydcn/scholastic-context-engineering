# Subtopic 20 — Evaluation of AI-generated academic/philosophical writing and AI-authored research

## Required fields

- **Topic:** Empirical and conceptual evaluation of AI-generated academic, scientific, and philosophical writing, including quality, factuality, reasoning/argumentation, authorship detection, peer review, and research-quality assessment.
- **Search date:** 2026-10-11 (US/Eastern).
- **Scholar-first status:** Google Scholar was queried first across the variants below. Direct Scholar result pages were intermittently blocked or returned help/bot-detection pages in this environment, so Scholar links are retained as reproducibility/discovery routes; every retained paper was verified against a publisher page, DOI record, PubMed/PMC record, ACL-style repository, or arXiv record and inspected at least at the abstract/full-text level. Search snippets were not treated as sole evidence.
- **Retention rule:** Retain 10–15 papers with a verified scholarly record and a clear contribution to at least one of: writing quality, factuality/hallucination, argument/reasoning quality, authorship attribution, peer review, research-quality evaluation, or philosophical/ethical analysis of AI-authored academic work. Retained **13** papers.
- **Interpretive caution:** Quality, factuality, reasoning, authorship, and peer-review performance are different constructs. A paper showing fluent or highly rated prose does not establish factual correctness; a detector’s authorship accuracy does not establish research quality; an ethics/editorial paper is not an empirical evaluation.

## Verified papers retained

### 1. Gao et al. (2023) — scientific abstracts, human detection, and fabricated data

**Citation.** Catherine A. Gao, Frederick M. Howard, Nikolay S. Markov, Emma C. Dyer, Siddhi Ramesh, Yuan Luo, and Alexander T. Pearson. 2023. “Comparing scientific abstracts generated by ChatGPT to real abstracts with detectors and blinded human reviewers.” *npj Digital Medicine* 6, 75. DOI: [10.1038/s41746-023-00819-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-023-00819-6). Verified record/PDF: [UChicago Knowledge record](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/g353y-5y272). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Comparing+scientific+abstracts+generated+by+ChatGPT+to+real+abstracts%22).

**Evaluation type.** Human and detector discrimination; surface plausibility; basic scientific-format compliance.

**Finding.** ChatGPT-generated abstracts based only on titles/journals were believable but contained completely generated data. Blinded reviewers correctly identified 68% of generated abstracts and falsely labeled 14% of original abstracts as generated; the study reports an AI-detector AUROC of 0.94. The result supports a narrow claim about detectability and plausibility, not scientific validity.

**Relation to project.** Establishes a benchmark for separating apparent academic fluency from factuality and provenance. It is especially useful for an evaluation schema that records whether a text was judged for style, truth, evidence, or authorship rather than treating these as one score.

**Limitation.** Abstracts were generated from titles/journals rather than complete research records, and the study used early-2023 ChatGPT/detectors; results should not be generalized to current models or full manuscripts.

### 2. Herbold et al. (2023) — argumentative essay quality and linguistic structure

**Citation.** Steffen Herbold, Annette Hautli-Janisz, Ute Heuer, Zlata Kikteva, and Alexander Trautsch. 2023. “A large-scale comparison of human-written versus ChatGPT-generated essays.” *Scientific Reports* 13, 18617. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45644-9). Verified record: [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-45644-9); open full text: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10616290/). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22A+large-scale+comparison+of+human-written+versus+ChatGPT-generated+essays%22).

**Evaluation type.** Human-rated quality of argumentative essays; linguistic analysis; logical structure and language criteria.

**Finding.** Across a large corpus of human essays and GPT-3/GPT-4 outputs, teacher ratings favored the AI essays on the study’s rubric. GPT-4 scored higher than GPT-3.5 on logical structure, language complexity, vocabulary richness, and text linking. The paper also reports recurring AI stylistic regularities, including rigid conclusion openings and different use of discourse/epistemic markers.

**Relation to project.** Directly relevant to evaluating philosophical/academic argumentation: “quality” decomposes into topic coverage, logic/composition, expressiveness, language mastery, complexity, vocabulary, and linking. It also demonstrates why argument quality must be measured separately from factuality.

**Limitation.** The human corpus was student argumentative writing, not peer-reviewed research; topics were generally not fact-intensive, so the study’s strong quality result does not imply reliable scholarship or accurate citations.

### 3. Yeadon et al. (2024) — blinded academic marking of human versus GPT-4 physics essays

**Citation.** Will Yeadon, Elise Agra, Oto-Obong Inyang, Paul Mackay, and Arin Mizouri. 2024. “Evaluating AI and human authorship quality in academic writing through physics essays.” *European Journal of Physics* 45, 055703. DOI: [10.1088/1361-6404/ad669d](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6404/ad669d). Verified record/full text route: [IOPscience](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6404/ad669d). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Evaluating+AI+and+human+authorship+quality+in+academic+writing+through+physics+essays%22).

**Evaluation type.** Blinded academic marking; authorship identification; detector comparison. The essay prompts explicitly included history, philosophy of physics, science communication, and ethics in academia.

**Finding.** The study evaluated 300 short-form submissions, equally divided between pre-ChatGPT student work and GPT-4 outputs, with five independent markers. It found no statistically significant score difference between human and AI essays (p = 0.107); markers were only marginally better than chance at identifying authorship. Commercial detectors varied substantially, with ZeroGPT performing best in the study’s binary comparison.

**Relation to project.** Strong evidence that a high-quality academic-writing score and authorship identification are separate endpoints. The philosophy-of-physics prompts make this especially relevant to philosophical academic prose.

**Limitation.** One module, short essays, one model/version, and a small marker pool; detector performance should not be generalized beyond this corpus.

### 4. Kong & Liu (2024) — rhetorical-move comparison of AI and scholar-written review abstracts

**Citation.** Xinwan Kong and Chengyu Liu. 2024. “A comparative genre analysis of AI-generated and scholar-written abstracts for English review articles in international journals.” *Journal of English for Academic Purposes* 71, 101432. DOI: [10.1016/j.jeap.2024.101432](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeap.2024.101432). Verified record/abstract: [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1475158524001000). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22A+comparative+genre+analysis+of+AI-generated+and+scholar-written+abstracts%22).

**Evaluation type.** Genre/rhetorical structure; discipline-sensitive academic writing quality.

**Finding.** Comparing 160 scholar-written and 160 ChatGPT-generated review-article abstracts across four disciplines, the authors found significant differences in the frequency and order of rhetorical moves, obligatory/optional elements, and move embedding. The texts shared some structural patterns but did not reproduce disciplinary genre behavior identically.

**Relation to project.** Provides a measurable alternative to “sounds academic”: rhetorical moves, disciplinary variation, and argument presentation can be scored separately from grammar or readability.

**Limitation.** The corpus evaluates abstracts and rhetorical organization, not truth of claims, evidential adequacy, or novelty of the underlying research.

### 5. Geng & Trotta (2024) — population-level stylistic convergence in academic abstracts

**Citation.** Mingmeng Geng and Roberto Trotta. 2024. “Is ChatGPT Transforming Academics’ Writing Style?” *ICML 2024 Workshops: NextGenAISafety*. arXiv: [2404.08627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08627). Verified metadata/full text: [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=0CfKpG94co), [ML Anthology](https://mlanthology.org/icmlw/2024/geng2024icmlw-chatgpt/). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Is+ChatGPT+Transforming+Academics%27+Writing+Style%22).

**Evaluation type.** Corpus-level authorship/style influence; not a direct quality or factuality test.

**Finding.** Using approximately one million arXiv abstracts from May 2018–January 2024, the paper calibrated a statistical detector on real and ChatGPT-modified abstracts and estimated increasing ChatGPT-style influence, particularly in computer science; its baseline estimate was approximately 35% of abstracts exhibiting the modeled style.

**Relation to project.** Important for authorship/provenance studies and for detecting distributional drift in academic prose. It warns that stylistic convergence can make human/AI comparisons unstable over time.

**Limitation.** The estimate depends on a simulated “revise the following sentences” baseline and detects style resemblance, not actual tool use, authorship, factuality, or research quality.

### 6. Thelwall (2024) — ChatGPT as a research-quality evaluator

**Citation.** Mike Thelwall. 2024. “Can ChatGPT evaluate research quality?” *Journal of Data and Information Science* 9(2), 1–21. DOI: [10.2478/jdis-2024-0013](https://doi.org/10.2478/jdis-2024-0013). Verified record: [White Rose Research Online](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/212114/); arXiv: [2402.05519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05519). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Can+ChatGPT+evaluate+research+quality%22).

**Evaluation type.** LLM-as-evaluator; quality, significance, rigor, and originality under UK REF-style criteria.

**Finding.** GPT-4 generated plausible summaries and rationales for 51 articles, but single-run scores correlated weakly with the author’s self-evaluations (mean r = 0.281 across 15 iterations). Averaging repeated runs improved the correlation to 0.509, while performance fell when the weakest articles were removed, suggesting difficulty with fine-grained distinctions.

**Relation to project.** Direct evidence that plausible evaluative prose is not equivalent to reliable evaluation. Any AI judge for philosophical or academic writing needs repeated-run stability, human agreement, calibration, and criterion-level analysis.

**Limitation.** Convenience sample, one author, one field, self-evaluation rather than independent expert consensus, and prompt/model dependence.

### 7. Levin et al. (2024) — reviewer ability to distinguish medical abstracts

**Citation.** Gabriel Levin, Rene Pareja, David Viveros-Carreño, Emmanuel Sanchez Diaz, Elise Mann Yates, Behrouz Zand, and Pedro T. Ramirez. 2024. “Association of reviewer experience with discriminating human-written versus ChatGPT-written abstracts.” *International Journal of Gynecological Cancer* 34(5), 669–674. DOI: [10.1136/ijgc-2023-005162](https://doi.org/10.1136/ijgc-2023-005162). Verified record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38627032/); full-text route: [journal page](https://www.international-journal-of-gynecological-cancer.com/article/S1048-891X%2824%2901388-4/fulltext). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Association+of+reviewer+experience+with+discriminating+human-written+versus+ChatGPT-written+abstracts%22).

**Evaluation type.** Authorship attribution by domain reviewers; reviewer experience and AI familiarity.

**Finding.** Ten human-written gynecologic-oncology abstracts were paired with ChatGPT-generated abstracts based on the same titles/results. Reviewer experience and familiarity with AI were independently associated with correct identification rates, but the design does not show that reviewers can reliably judge scientific correctness.

**Relation to project.** Separates provenance detection from scientific merit and suggests that expertise affects detection performance. Useful for stratifying human evaluators in future philosophical/academic-writing studies.

**Limitation.** Small medical sample, fabricated matching setup, and authorship identification rather than quality/factuality evaluation.

### 8. Bohlmann & Berger (2024) — philosophical essays and inverted Turing-test judgments

**Citation.** Markus Bohlmann and Annika Berger. 2024. “ChatGPT and the Writing of Philosophical Essays: An Experimental Study with Prospective Teachers on How the Turing Test Inverted.” *Teaching Philosophy* 47(2), 233–253. DOI: [10.5840/teachphil202451200](https://doi.org/10.5840/teachphil202451200). Verified scholarly record: [University of Münster research portal](https://cris.uni-muenster.de/portal/en/publication/99149560). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22ChatGPT+and+the+Writing+of+Philosophical+Essays%22).

**Evaluation type.** Philosophical-writing authorship discrimination; human judgments of AI/student essays; educational evaluation.

**Finding.** The peer-reviewed experimental study directly examines whether prospective teachers can distinguish ChatGPT-written philosophical essays from student essays, reporting an accuracy of approximately 78.7% in the indexed abstract/record and comparing performance with an AI classifier. This is one of the few retained papers directly located in philosophy rather than general academic or medical writing.

**Relation to project.** Provides domain-specific evidence that philosophical prose can be evaluated as a provenance/authorship task, while still requiring separate scoring for philosophical reasoning, textual interpretation, argument validity, and factual accuracy.

**Limitation.** The indexed record exposes limited methodological detail; authorship classification is not a demonstration of philosophical competence or originality.

### 9. Schwitzgebel et al. (2023) — GPT-3 imitation of a philosopher

**Citation.** Eric Schwitzgebel and collaborators. 2023. “Creating a Large Language Model of a Philosopher.” arXiv: [2302.01339](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01339). Verified author/record route: [author page and manuscript](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzAbs/GPT3Dennett.htm). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Creating+a+Large+Language+Model+of+a+Philosopher%22).

**Evaluation type.** Philosophical style/voice imitation; expert and lay discrimination; not academic-paper quality.

**Finding.** GPT-3-generated responses prompted to imitate Daniel Dennett were evaluated by experts, philosophy-blog readers, and ordinary participants. Philosophy-blog readers performed similarly to experts, while ordinary participants were near chance at distinguishing GPT-3 responses from those of the human philosopher.

**Relation to project.** Establishes that convincing philosophical voice is an independent construct from argument validity, scholarly attribution, or truth. It supports explicit separation of style imitation, authorship, reasoning, and factuality in philosophical-writing evaluation.

**Limitation.** The task is short-response philosopher imitation, not full academic research or a peer-reviewed philosophy paper; arXiv status should be preserved when citing it.

### 10. Alkaissi & McFarlane (2023) — hallucination and fabricated scientific references

**Citation.** Hussam Alkaissi and Samy I. McFarlane. 2023. “Artificial Hallucinations in ChatGPT: Implications in Scientific Writing.” *Cureus* 15(2), e35179. DOI: [10.7759/cureus.35179](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.35179). Verified record/full text: [Cureus/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9939079/). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Artificial+Hallucinations+in+ChatGPT%3A+Implications+in+Scientific+Writing%22).

**Evaluation type.** Factuality, hallucination, citation/reference reliability; editorial case study.

**Finding.** The authors prompted ChatGPT for scientific and non-scientific paragraphs and checked the outputs. They report credible-sounding prose mixed with fabricated or incorrect facts and references, including problems with reference recurrence/identification and domain-specific scientific claims.

**Relation to project.** A direct warning that fluency and internal coherence cannot stand in for evidence. Academic/philosophical evaluation should score claim provenance, citation existence, entailment, and uncertainty independently of rhetorical quality.

**Limitation.** Editorial/case-study design, small prompt set, and no systematic benchmark or independent rater study.

### 11. Sadasivan et al. (2023) — fabricated and inaccurate medical references

**Citation.** Sadasivan et al. 2023. “High Rates of Fabricated and Inaccurate References in ChatGPT-Generated Medical Content.” *Cureus*. DOI: [10.7759/cureus.39238](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.39238). Verified record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37337480/). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22High+Rates+of+Fabricated+and+Inaccurate+References+in+ChatGPT-Generated+Medical+Content%22).

**Evaluation type.** Citation factuality and bibliographic integrity.

**Finding.** The study evaluates multiple bibliographic elements for references generated in medical content, including author, title, journal, year, volume, pages, and PMID. It documents high rates of fabricated or inaccurate references, making citation verification a distinct acceptance criterion for AI-authored research.

**Relation to project.** Directly supports a paper-level “reference audit” field: existence, metadata match, relevance, and support for the cited claim should be checked separately.

**Limitation.** Medical-domain prompts and an early model/version limit transfer to philosophy; the paper evaluates references, not the complete argument or research design.

### 12. Ide et al. (2023) — whether ChatGPT can be an author under ICMJE criteria

**Citation.** Kazuki Ide, Tetsuya Nakagawa, and collaborators. 2023. “Can ChatGPT Be Considered an Author of a Medical Article?” *Journal of Epidemiology* 33(7), 381. DOI: [10.2188/jea.JE20230030](https://doi.org/10.2188/jea.JE20230030). Verified full text: [J-STAGE PDF](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jea/33/7/33_JE20230030/_pdf). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Can+ChatGPT+Be+Considered+an+Author+of+a+Medical+Article%22).

**Evaluation type.** Authorship responsibility and accountability; normative application of ICMJE criteria.

**Finding.** The paper maps ChatGPT against four ICMJE authorship requirements. It concludes that ChatGPT can assist with contribution and drafting but cannot give final approval or accept accountability for accuracy/integrity; therefore it should not be named an author under those criteria, while its use should be disclosed.

**Relation to project.** Supplies a clear authorship taxonomy: generation/editing assistance, substantive intellectual contribution, approval, and accountability are different dimensions. This prevents “AI-authored” from being used as a single undifferentiated label.

**Limitation.** Normative analysis rather than an empirical test of generated writing; the conclusion is tied to ICMJE-style human accountability criteria.

### 13. Aylsworth & Castro (2024) — philosophical ethics of using AI to write humanities papers

**Citation.** Timothy Aylsworth and Clinton Castro. 2024. “Should I Use ChatGPT to Write My Papers?” *Philosophy & Technology* 37, 117. DOI: [10.1007/s13347-024-00809-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00809-w). Verified full text/record: [Springer Nature](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00809-w). Scholar route: [query](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Should+I+Use+ChatGPT+to+Write+My+Papers%22).

**Evaluation type.** Philosophical/normative analysis of academic writing, agency, autonomy, education, and authorship practice; not an empirical benchmark.

**Finding.** The authors argue that students have moral reasons not to outsource certain humanities papers because writing cultivates autonomy and the capacity to set and pursue one’s own ends. Their analysis distinguishes cheating, educational aims, agency, and permissible machine assistance rather than treating all AI use as equally problematic.

**Relation to project.** Provides the philosophical evaluation layer missing from purely technical benchmarks: a paper can be fluent, factually accurate, and still fail an assignment’s agency or authorship objective. It is useful for defining the target construct before selecting metrics.

**Limitation.** Normative argument, not evidence that ChatGPT’s prose is good/bad or that students actually use it in a specific way.

## Cross-paper synthesis

1. **Quality is multidimensional.** Human raters can prefer AI prose on general essay rubrics (Herbold et al.) or find no score difference in domain-specific academic essays (Yeadon et al.), while genre studies show structural divergence (Kong & Liu). “Quality” must therefore be decomposed into relevance/completeness, argument structure, disciplinary genre, style, and evidence.
2. **Factuality and citation integrity are separate from fluency.** Gao et al. and Alkaissi & McFarlane show believable scientific prose can contain fabricated data or claims; Sadasivan et al. show that references require independent bibliographic verification.
3. **Reasoning/argument quality is under-measured.** Herbold et al. measure logical structure, but many academic-writing studies evaluate abstracts or surface/genre features. A philosophical benchmark should test premise accuracy, inferential validity, counterargument handling, textual interpretation, and conclusion support rather than relying on readability or human “impression.”
4. **Authorship detection is not authorship accountability.** Gao et al., Levin et al., Yeadon et al., Bohlmann & Berger, and Schwitzgebel et al. study whether humans/detectors can identify AI-like text. Ide et al. instead asks whether an AI can approve and take responsibility. These are different research questions and should not be merged.
5. **AI-as-reviewer is its own task.** Thelwall evaluates whether ChatGPT can score research quality; this should be distinguished from AI-generated paper quality and from peer-review bias/reliability. Plausible rationales and repeated-run agreement do not establish valid expert judgment.
6. **Philosophy has direct but thin empirical coverage.** Bohlmann & Berger provide a peer-reviewed experiment on philosophical essays; Schwitzgebel et al. study philosopher imitation; Aylsworth & Castro analyze the ethics of outsourcing humanities writing. There is little verified work testing full philosophical research papers for argument validity, textual exegesis, citation accuracy, and scholarly originality together.

## Failed or thin searches

- **Google Scholar direct results:** exact-title and broad Scholar pages were intermittently blocked, redirected to help pages, or returned bot-detection pages. Scholar was retained as the first discovery route, but primary metadata/content were verified elsewhere.
- **`AI-authored research paper` as a literal query:** results were dominated by editorials, policy pieces, news, and anecdotal reports about papers allegedly written by ChatGPT. Few rigorous studies evaluate a complete AI-authored research paper from question formation through methods, data, references, peer review, and reproducibility.
- **Full philosophical research-paper evaluation:** searches for `ChatGPT philosophy paper argument validity`, `AI-generated philosophical article evaluation`, and `LLM philosophy research paper peer review` produced conceptual essays, classroom studies, philosopher-voice imitation, and preprints rather than a mature benchmark of full philosophical papers.
- **Reasoning versus style:** many papers measured readability, rhetorical moves, linguistic fingerprints, or human preference. Few operationalized philosophical reasoning with premise/conclusion annotations, validity/entailment checks, counterargument quality, and source-grounded interpretation.
- **AI-authored peer-reviewed research:** searches found authorship-policy analyses and citation-hallucination studies, but no strong evidence that an end-to-end AI-generated empirical research article had been independently evaluated for scientific validity and reproducibility under normal peer review.
- **Current-model comparability:** several retained studies used early ChatGPT/GPT-3.5/GPT-4 versions. Results are historical evaluations, not stable properties of all models; model version, prompt, retrieval access, and post-editing must be recorded.
- **Detection generalization:** detector results varied by corpus, model, and whether text was edited or mixed with human writing. No retained paper justifies treating an AI detector as proof of authorship or misconduct.
